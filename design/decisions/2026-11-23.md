## 159. What a server needs of the library, second: a list of borrows, a reader that lends slices, `writev`

Approved on 22 November 2026, after slice 158:

1731. **One slice.** First `List<read T>` with tests of its own, then the reader, then `writev`, and last the two
     servers moved to the reader and measured.
1732. **`List<read T>`.** Only `List`, and only `read`: `List<ref T>` and borrows in a map, a set or an array come
     later. The rules are those of a list of a type that carries a borrow, which held already: the list follows
     every source it borrowed from, each on its own, and changing a source or letting it end while the list is
     used later is an error. It does not go to another task or on the heap as `shared`, and it goes into a field
     or out of a function only with `from`. In C it is a list of pointers; a `read string` or a `read byte[]` is
     kept as the pair of a start and a length. As Rust's `Vec<&'a T>`, without one lifetime for all: each source
     is followed on its own.
1733. **`xs.clear()` lets go of every borrow the list holds**, on a list in a local variable: a list of pieces
     made once outside a loop is filled again in every round, with no memory taken for each request. Rust cannot
     do this (the lifetime of a `Vec<&'a T>` is fixed); there one makes a new `Vec` each round or uses a trick.
     It costs one rule of the checker.
1734. **The reader in three calls.** `await reader.fillAsync(read token)` gives a `Result<bool, IoError>`, `false`
     when the other side has closed; `reader.buffered()` lends the block as a `read byte[]`, and is not async;
     `reader.consume(n)`. Both forms, async and not. Three calls because an async function cannot give a borrow
     (388): Rust's one call, `fill_buf`, which gives a borrow, cannot be written for an async reader. As Zig
     0.15's `fill`, `buffered` and `toss`, and Go's `Peek`, `Buffered` and `Discard`.
1735. **`fill` always reads more.** It adds one more read to the block even when the block holds bytes, as C#'s
     `PipeReader` does: a parser that found half a message does not go round for nothing. Rust's `fill_buf`
     gives the block at once when it is not empty, a loop that spins on half a request.
1736. **The block of a reader of the network starts at 4 KB** and doubles as it fills, up to the limit (64 KB if
     none is given); a fill with the block full at the limit gives a new `IoError.TooLong` ("too long"), and
     `LineTooLong` stays for lines. A reader of a file keeps its block of 64 KB. A reader of the network took
     64 KB for each connection, which is why the servers kept buffers of 4 KB of their own.
1737. **`consume(n)` with `n` larger than `buffered().length()` is a panic** (Rust cuts it short without a word,
     Go gives an error). `readLine` and `readSome` go on from the same block.
1738. **`writev` is `conn.sendPieces(read List<read byte[]>)`** and `sendPiecesAsync(..., token)`. It sends all of
     them: a write that sends a part is followed by the rest, and more than 1,024 pieces go in several calls. On
     Linux and macOS the list's memory is given to the system as it is, as an array of `iovec`, since a window
     is laid out as one; on Windows it is copied into an array of `WSABUF`, whose fields are the other way round.
1739. **The servers.** The store and the HTTP server read through the reader (`fill`, `buffered`, `consume`)
     where they kept a buffer of their own, about 20 lines fewer. `writev` has no natural place in either: in the
     store a value is read under the lock of its map, and the HTTP server's body is 4 bytes; this is said.
1740. **Measured.** (a) The two servers before and after on Linux, run in turn four times: 50 and 1,000
     connections, and the memory of 100,000 idle ones. (b) `writev` over the loopback: a head of 100 bytes and a
     body of 4 KB and 64 KB, sent after copying into one buffer, with `sendPieces`, and with C's `writev`, in
     [the library's numbers](../../benchmarks/library.md#net). Shown to the author before anything is committed.
1741. **Left out:** borrows in a map, a set or an array, `List<ref T>`, `writev` on a writer of a file, a block
     let go of while a connection is idle (the pool of Go's `net/http`), and `sendfile`.

From the slice, found while the list of borrows was built, approved on 23 November:

1742. **On a list of borrows `get` and `removeLast` give the borrow itself, or `null`: a `read T?`**, not a borrow
     of a borrow. What `removeLast` takes out is no longer in the list, so it borrows what the list borrowed and
     the list may change while it is used; this holds for a list of a type that carries a borrow too, which
     only lets programs through that were refused.
1743. **A list of windows has no `get` and no `removeLast`.** They would give a `read byte[]?`, and a slice that
     may be missing is not in the language; the error says to test the index against `length()` and take
     `xs[i]`.
1744. **The searches of a list of borrows take a borrow**: `numbers.indexOf(read 9)`, as a parameter that is a
     `read int` does; a text constant goes as it is (1403).
1745. **A loop that changes a source the list still borrows from the round before is told to clear the list
     earlier**: the hint of the error is `pieces.clear()` in every round before the line. *Replaced by 1748 on
     23 November.*

What the list of borrows was tested with: `ok_list_of_borrows` runs every method of the list on texts, structs,
numbers and windows, a function that gives a list of borrows with `from`, a struct that holds one, a list
cleared and filled again in a loop around an `await`, and gives the same in the interpreter and compiled, under
the sanitizers and with no memory left behind. Eight programs that are refused show the errors: a source
changed, a list cleared late in a round or only in some rounds, a list given to a task, a list given back
without `from`, `get` on a list of windows and a list of `ref` borrows.

From the slice, found while the reader and the send of several pieces were built. 1748 was approved on 23
November; the others wait for approval:

1746. **The input and its block** (open). `Stdin` is one reader that every task has, and its block is shared:
     while one task looks at what `buffered()` lent, another task's read of `Stdin` may move the bytes of the
     block or read new ones over them. Nothing is read outside the block, which never moves or ends, but the
     bytes change under a `read` borrow. Proposed: the first task that calls `fill`, `buffered` or `consume` on
     the input holds it, and a read of the input by another task after that is a panic that says so; one
     comparison in each read of the input, and nothing on any other reader.
1747. **The limit of `fill`.** `fill` on a block that holds the limit or more is `TooLong`, and does not stay:
     once `consume` gives out of the block, `fill` reads again. The limit is the longest line of the reader of a
     connection, and 64 KB for a file, `Stdin` and the pipe of another program, whose readers have no limit of
     their own. A read may bring the block past the limit (a connection whose limit is 16 bytes is read 4 KB at
     a time); the next `fill` is then `TooLong`.
1748. **The borrows of a list count until it is cleared, and a `clear` reads none of them**, in place of 1745.
     A use of the list is no error when a `clear` of it lies on every way to the use from where a source it
     borrowed from changed or ended: in the same round between the two, or in the next round before the use,
     and not in a branch or a loop the use is outside of. One rule for a source that changes and for one that
     is gone at the end of a round, the natural shape of a server that builds a head in each round; the order
     of `clear` against the change no longer matters, and the error says to clear the list before it is used
     again. It found its way from a narrower rule, for a value that is gone only, which left the two cases
     apart. Approved on 23 November 2026, in place of 1745: the safety is the same, the rule one sentence,
     and nothing is spent when the program runs.
1749. **The interpreter sends the pieces as one run of bytes.** What goes over the connection is the same; the
     interpreter does not call `writev`.

What the reader was tested with: `ok_reader_fill` reads lines found in the block of a file and of the input,
the end, `readLine` after `consume`, and `TooLong` on a block of 64 KB that `consume` ends; `ok_net_reader_fill`
reads messages framed by their length through `fillAsync`, one of 5,000 bytes in three pieces that grows the
block past its first 4 KB, one cut inside its length, `TooLong` at a limit of 16 bytes and a cancel of
`fillAsync`. `ok_net_send_pieces` sends with and without async: a list cleared and filled in each round, empty
pieces and an empty list, 3,000 pieces of one byte, two pieces of a million bytes and more around a mark, and a
cancelled token. Both give the same in the interpreter and compiled. A panic shows `consume` past the block,
and eight programs that are refused show the errors: a view used after `consume` or `fill`, `consume` through
`read`, `consumeAsync`, `fill` and `sendPieces` without async in an async function, a list of bytes given to
`sendPieces`, and a list cleared in one branch only or before the value was borrowed. `ok_list_of_borrows_gone_cleared`
and `ok_list_of_borrows_cleared_after_change` (what `error_list_of_borrows_cleared_late` was) show 1748.

Measured (1740), on Linux in the virtual machine of the servers, the servers as they were and as they are
built by one compiler and run in turn four times, medians: the store with 50 connections 1,358,553 answers a
second where it got 1,366,245 (p99 72 µs, 73 before), with 1,000 connections 1,262,719 where 1,262,463 (p99
1.09 ms, 1.08), the HTTP server 1,232,847 where 1,216,697, and 4.95 KB for each of 100,000 idle connections
where 4.98. A first build lost 7 to 10% of the answers and held 5.19 KB for an idle connection: `fillAsync` tried
a receive before every wait, where `receiveAsync` waits for the loop at once when the last receive emptied the
connection, and kept a room for its wait in the frame beside the one the calls of the network share. Now the
reader of a connection fills its block as `receiveAsync` receives, in that one room, and the fields of a reader
lie with no room between them (72 bytes, 96 before); neither changes what a program does. A head of 100 bytes
and a body of 64 KB go out with `sendPieces` in 5.38 µs, where a copy into one buffer and `send` take 8.92 µs
and C's `writev` 5.56 µs; with a body of 4 KB the four ways are within 3% ([the
servers](../../benchmarks/server.md#a-reader-that-lends-its-block), [the
library](../../benchmarks/library.md#several-pieces-at-once)).

Found while the slice was tested, under the load of the tests of macOS and Linux run at once: a task of a
`TaskGroup` (158) told its group it had ended, let go of the group's lock and only then was freed, so a
`whenAll` could see the count fall, the program end and the count of its blocks find the task still taken
(one run in ten to twenty of `ok_task_group_wait` with eight at once on Linux; none in 1,600 since). The task
is now freed under the lock. And `ok_udp_async` let a port go before it made the socket that was to send to
it, so the system could give that port to the socket itself, which then heard its own datagram; the socket is
made first now.
