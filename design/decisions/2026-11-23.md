## 159. What a server needs of the library, second: a list of borrows, a reader that lends slices, `writev`

Approved on 22 November 2026, after slice 158:

1731. **One slice.** First `List<read T>` with tests of its own, then the reader, then `writev`, and last the two
     servers moved to the reader and measured.
1732. **`List<read T>`.** Only `List`, and only `read`: `List<ref T>` and borrows in a map, a set or an array come
     later. The rules are those of a list of a type that carries a borrow, which held already: the list follows
     every source it borrowed from, each on its own, and changing a source or letting it end while the list is
     used later is an error. It does not go to another task or on the heap as `shared`, and it goes into a field
     or out of a function only with `from`. In C it is a list of pointers; a `read string` or a `read byte[]` is
     kept as the pair of a start and a length. As Rust's `Vec<&'a T>`, without one lifetime for all: each source
     is followed on its own.
1733. **`xs.clear()` lets go of every borrow the list holds**, on a list in a local variable: a list of pieces
     made once outside a loop is filled again in every round, with no memory taken for each request. Rust cannot
     do this (the lifetime of a `Vec<&'a T>` is fixed); there one makes a new `Vec` each round or uses a trick.
     It costs one rule of the checker.
1734. **The reader in three calls.** `await reader.fillAsync(read token)` gives a `Result<bool, IoError>`, `false`
     when the other side has closed; `reader.buffered()` lends the block as a `read byte[]`, and is not async;
     `reader.consume(n)`. Both forms, async and not. Three calls because an async function cannot give a borrow
     (388): Rust's one call, `fill_buf`, which gives a borrow, cannot be written for an async reader. As Zig
     0.15's `fill`, `buffered` and `toss`, and Go's `Peek`, `Buffered` and `Discard`.
1735. **`fill` always reads more.** It adds one more read to the block even when the block holds bytes, as C#'s
     `PipeReader` does: a parser that found half a message does not go round for nothing. Rust's `fill_buf`
     gives the block at once when it is not empty, a loop that spins on half a request.
1736. **The block of a reader of the network starts at 4 KB** and doubles as it fills, up to the limit (64 KB if
     none is given); a fill with the block full at the limit gives a new `IoError.TooLong` ("too long"), and
     `LineTooLong` stays for lines. A reader of a file keeps its block of 64 KB. A reader of the network took
     64 KB for each connection, which is why the servers kept buffers of 4 KB of their own.
1737. **`consume(n)` with `n` larger than `buffered().length()` is a panic** (Rust cuts it short without a word,
     Go gives an error). `readLine` and `readSome` go on from the same block.
1738. **`writev` is `conn.sendPieces(read List<read byte[]>)`** and `sendPiecesAsync(..., token)`. It sends all of
     them: a write that sends a part is followed by the rest, and more than 1,024 pieces go in several calls. On
     Linux and macOS the list's memory is given to the system as it is, as an array of `iovec`, since a window
     is laid out as one; on Windows it is copied into an array of `WSABUF`, whose fields are the other way round.
1739. **The servers.** The store and the HTTP server read through the reader (`fill`, `buffered`, `consume`)
     where they kept a buffer of their own, about 20 lines fewer. `writev` has no natural place in either: in the
     store a value is read under the lock of its map, and the HTTP server's body is 4 bytes; this is said.
1740. **Measured.** (a) The two servers before and after on Linux, run in turn four times: 50 and 1,000
     connections, and the memory of 100,000 idle ones. (b) `writev` over the loopback: a head of 100 bytes and a
     body of 4 KB and 64 KB, sent after copying into one buffer, with `sendPieces`, and with C's `writev`, in
     [the library's numbers](../../benchmarks/library.md#net). Shown to the author before anything is committed.
1741. **Left out:** borrows in a map, a set or an array, `List<ref T>`, `writev` on a writer of a file, a block
     let go of while a connection is idle (the pool of Go's `net/http`), and `sendfile`.

From the slice, found while the list of borrows was built, approved on 23 November:

1742. **On a list of borrows `get` and `removeLast` give the borrow itself, or `null`: a `read T?`**, not a borrow
     of a borrow. What `removeLast` takes out is no longer in the list, so it borrows what the list borrowed and
     the list may change while it is used; this holds for a list of a type that carries a borrow too, which
     only lets programs through that were refused.
1743. **A list of windows has no `get` and no `removeLast`.** They would give a `read byte[]?`, and a slice that
     may be missing is not in the language; the error says to test the index against `length()` and take
     `xs[i]`.
1744. **The searches of a list of borrows take a borrow**: `numbers.indexOf(read 9)`, as a parameter that is a
     `read int` does; a text constant goes as it is (1403).
1745. **A loop that changes a source the list still borrows from the round before is told to clear the list
     earlier**: the hint of the error is `pieces.clear()` in every round before the line. *Replaced by 1748 on
     23 November.*

What the list of borrows was tested with: `ok_list_of_borrows` runs every method of the list on texts, structs,
numbers and windows, a function that gives a list of borrows with `from`, a struct that holds one, a list
cleared and filled again in a loop around an `await`, and gives the same in the interpreter and compiled, under
the sanitizers and with no memory left behind. Eight programs that are refused show the errors: a source
changed, a list cleared late in a round or only in some rounds, a list given to a task, a list given back
without `from`, `get` on a list of windows and a list of `ref` borrows.

From the slice, found while the reader and the send of several pieces were built, approved on 23 November
(1748 first, the others after the slice):

1746. **The first task that looks at the block of the input holds it.** `Stdin` is one reader that every task
     has, and its block is shared: while one task looks at what `buffered()` lent, another task's read of
     `Stdin` could move the bytes of the block or read new ones over them. Nothing was read outside the block,
     which never moves or ends, but the bytes changed under a `read` borrow. Now the first task that calls
     `fill`, `buffered` or `consume` on the input (the program itself, outside any task, counts as one) holds
     it, and a read of the input by any other task after that is a panic that says so. Tasks that only read
     lines go on as before until one looks at the block. The claim is made under the lock of the input, so a
     read another task has begun ends first, and an async read looks again each time it goes on after a
     wait, so one that waited while the input was claimed stops before it touches the block. What it costs:
     in a program with tasks, one comparison of the reader with the input in each read, and a load of a flag
     in each read of the input; the task's mark lies in room its record had spare. A program without tasks
     has no check.
1747. **The limit of `fill`.** `fill` on a block that holds the limit or more is `TooLong`, and does not stay:
     once `consume` gives out of the block, `fill` reads again. The limit is the longest line of the reader of a
     connection, and 64 KB for a file, `Stdin` and the pipe of another program, whose readers have no limit of
     their own. A read may bring the block past the limit (a connection whose limit is 16 bytes is read 4 KB at
     a time); the next `fill` is then `TooLong`.
1748. **The borrows of a list count until it is cleared, and a `clear` reads none of them**, in place of 1745.
     A use of the list is no error when a `clear` of it lies on every way to the use from where a source it
     borrowed from changed or ended: in the same round between the two, or in the next round before the use,
     and not in a branch or a loop the use is outside of. One rule for a source that changes and for one that
     is gone at the end of a round, the natural shape of a server that builds a head in each round; the order
     of `clear` against the change no longer matters, and the error says to clear the list before it is used
     again. It found its way from a narrower rule, for a value that is gone only, which left the two cases
     apart. Approved on 23 November 2026, in place of 1745: the safety is the same, the rule one sentence,
     and nothing is spent when the program runs.
1749. **The interpreter sends the pieces as one run of bytes.** What goes over the connection is the same; the
     interpreter does not call `writev`.

What the reader was tested with: `ok_reader_fill` reads lines found in the block of a file and of the input,
the end, `readLine` after `consume`, and `TooLong` on a block of 64 KB that `consume` ends; `ok_net_reader_fill`
reads messages framed by their length through `fillAsync`, one of 5,000 bytes in three pieces that grows the
block past its first 4 KB, one cut inside its length, `TooLong` at a limit of 16 bytes and a cancel of
`fillAsync`. `ok_net_send_pieces` sends with and without async: a list cleared and filled in each round, empty
pieces and an empty list, 3,000 pieces of one byte, two pieces of a million bytes and more around a mark, and a
cancelled token. Both give the same in the interpreter and compiled. A panic shows `consume` past the block,
and eight programs that are refused show the errors: a view used after `consume` or `fill`, `consume` through
`read`, `consumeAsync`, `fill` and `sendPieces` without async in an async function, a list of bytes given to
`sendPieces`, and a list cleared in one branch only or before the value was borrowed. `ok_list_of_borrows_gone_cleared`
and `ok_list_of_borrows_cleared_after_change` (what `error_list_of_borrows_cleared_late` was) show 1748.
`ok_stdin_held` reads lines of the input from two tasks in turn, then one that holds it reads it with
`buffered`, `consume`, `fillAsync`, `readLineAsync` and `readTextAsync`; `panic_stdin_held` shows an async read
of the input by a task after the program looked at the block, and `panic_stdin_held_thread` the lines of the
input read on a thread of its own after the program filled it (1746). Both give the same in the interpreter
and compiled.

Measured (1740), on Linux in the virtual machine of the servers, the servers as they were and as they are
built by one compiler and run in turn four times, medians: the store with 50 connections 1,358,553 answers a
second where it got 1,366,245 (p99 72 µs, 73 before), with 1,000 connections 1,262,719 where 1,262,463 (p99
1.09 ms, 1.08), the HTTP server 1,232,847 where 1,216,697, and 4.95 KB for each of 100,000 idle connections
where 4.98. A first build lost 7 to 10% of the answers and held 5.19 KB for an idle connection: `fillAsync` tried
a receive before every wait, where `receiveAsync` waits for the loop at once when the last receive emptied the
connection, and kept a room for its wait in the frame beside the one the calls of the network share. Now the
reader of a connection fills its block as `receiveAsync` receives, in that one room, and the fields of a reader
lie with no room between them (72 bytes, 96 before); neither changes what a program does. A head of 100 bytes
and a body of 64 KB go out with `sendPieces` in 5.38 µs, where a copy into one buffer and `send` take 8.92 µs
and C's `writev` 5.56 µs; with a body of 4 KB the four ways are within 3% ([the
servers](../../benchmarks/server.md#a-reader-that-lends-its-block), [the
library](../../benchmarks/library.md#several-pieces-at-once)).

Found while the slice was tested, under the load of the tests of macOS and Linux run at once: a task of a
`TaskGroup` (158) told its group it had ended, let go of the group's lock and only then was freed, so a
`whenAll` could see the count fall, the program end and the count of its blocks find the task still taken
(one run in ten to twenty of `ok_task_group_wait` with eight at once on Linux; none in 1,600 since). The task
is now freed under the lock. And `ok_udp_async` let a port go before it made the socket that was to send to
it, so the system could give that port to the socket itself, which then heard its own datagram; the socket is
made first now.

## 160. What the servers stand, and where they break

Approved on 23 November 2026, before the slice (1750 to 1760), and while it was built (1761 to 1764):

1750. **The subject.** What the servers of the server lane stand: each scenario a test in the repository; a small
     fault found is mended in the same slice, a change of the language or its library is a choice of its own.
     HTTP/1.1 in full comes later.
1751. **The scenarios**, each for the store (RESP) and the HTTP server: a) a limit of 256 descriptors and 1,000
     clients: the server neither ends nor spins, and answers new clients once the others leave; b) 1,000
     connections reset at random points (before a request, in the middle of one, while the answer is awaited,
     in the middle of an answer of 1 MB), the descriptors back to where they were; c) the sending half closed
     after the requests: every answer, then the close; d) a request a byte at a time, the answers read a byte
     at a time through a small receive buffer, a value of 1 MB checked byte by byte; e) 100 clients that ask
     for 1 MB and do not read: the memory of the server bounded, the others answered, `--idle-limit` cuts them;
     f) the trickle, for whether the answers are right; g) SIGTERM with 5,000 connections in the middle of a
     request: the server ends with 0 within `--grace` milliseconds, with no leak under the sanitizers (the
     servers of the trial get `--grace`; the others are not compared there); h) 10,000 requests that are not
     the protocol, drawn from a fixed seed (lengths too long, `*1000000`, halves), under the address and
     undefined-behaviour sanitizers: no crash, no hang, each answered or closed within a second, and a plain
     client answered after each round; i) 100 local ports: a connect gives an `IoError` and the program goes
     on (left out where the range cannot be narrowed).
1752. **Where.** Modes of `load.c`, and `tests/cli.rs` `the_servers_stand` on Unix, the servers built for release
     and under the sanitizers; compiled code only. *(1761 moves them to a program of their own.)*
1753. **Standing.** No crash, panic or hang; every answer right; the descriptors back to the base once the
     clients are gone; the peak of memory in the second round within 10% of the first (a C library keeps
     what it was given). *(1762 and 1763 change what memory is held to.)*
1754. **A job on Linux in the continuous integration**: `ubuntu-latest`, the whole `cargo test --release`, for
     whether it is right only.
1755. **The others too.** The servers of Rust, Zig and C through `measure.py --robust` in the image, the results
     published, what they lose included; not in the continuous integration.
1756. **A page**, `benchmarks/robustness.md` (and the site): the scenarios, the results of SG and the others, and
     what is not tried (a limit of memory by cgroup, TLS, a load over IPv6, Windows, a network that loses
     packets, io_uring).
1757. **The long run** stays at night on this machine, not in the continuous integration; the page links to the
     last one.
1758. **The order.** The stand and its test, then the faults mended, then the job on Linux, then the others
     measured, then the page and the documents; a measurement is shown before it is committed.
1759. **Two levels.** Quick, in `cargo test` and the continuous integration: 10,000 connections on Linux, fewer on
     macOS, 100,000 in all. Heavy, `measure.py --robust` in the image: resets 100,000 at once and a million in
     all, 100,000 clients beside a limit of 1,024 descriptors, 10,000 that do not read, SIGTERM with 50,000
     open, a million requests that are not the protocol, a byte at a time 2,000 at once and 100,000 in all;
     after each heavy run the descriptors and the memory of a second round.
1760. **The breaking point**: `measure.py --ramp`. Each connection asks once every 20 ms, counted from when the
     request was due; stages of 10 seconds with more load in each; an answer later than 100 ms fails; the
     ramp stops after two stages answered less than half. For each stage the requests sent and answered a
     second, p50, p99, p99.9 and the most, the share in time, the connections refused, the server's processor,
     memory, threads and descriptors, and the load's own processor; the knee (p99 ten times the first stage's or
     100 ms), the saturation (less than 95% of what was sent answered) and where less than 99.9% is in time.
     The store and the HTTP server against Rust, Zig and C, on Linux in the image and then on macOS; a line on
     the terminal for each stage; the results as tables.
1761. **The stand is a program of its own**, `trials/server/load/stand.c`, not modes of `load.c`: it starts the
     server, gives it its limit of descriptors, sends it SIGTERM, counts its descriptors and its memory, and
     holds every answer to what it must be; `load.c` stays the measure of speed, and the test runs the stand.
1762. **Memory, as measured.** The first round warms the server up and there are four: the last within 10% and
     2 MB of the second. Measured over eight rounds on Linux, a server's memory rises 15 to 35% in the first two
     (the C library), then lies flat; a leak of 100 bytes a connection, about a megabyte a round with 10,000
     connections, is still found.
1763. **Memory held idle, not the peak.** What the rule of 1762 looks at is the memory the server holds after
     each round, its connections gone (`VmRSS`, the footprint on macOS); the peak is told beside it. Over ten
     rounds of resets the peak of the HTTP server rose in steps, 6, 9, 11 MB, and lay flat, as the blocks of
     500 connections at once happened to meet; what leaks stays in the memory held idle.
1764. **`IoError.AddressNotAvailable`**, written `address not available`: an address that is not one of the
     machine's (`bind` on 192.0.2.1), or no local port left to connect from (`EADDRNOTAVAIL`,
     `WSAEADDRNOTAVAIL`; Rust's `ErrorKind::AddrNotAvailable`). It was `Other(99)`, `system error 99`, a
     number that differs between the systems (49 on macOS), where the network's other errors have names.
     The last variant, so the others keep their numbers; nothing more to pay when it runs.

Built: `trials/server/load/stand.c`, the stand of 1761, with the scenarios a to h of 1751 (`answers`, `fds`,
`reset`, `half`, `split`, `trickle`, `noread`, `term`, `fuzz`); `tests/cli.rs` `the_servers_stand`, its quick level,
against SG's two servers built for release and under the sanitizers, 10,000 connections on Linux and a tenth on
macOS; `.github/workflows/linux.yml` (1754); `measure.py --robust` with the heavy level of 1759 (1755), its counts
smaller with `ROBUST_SCALE`; `load.c --ramp` and `measure.py --ramp` (1760). The quick level holds the servers to
all of 1753 but the rule of rounds for memory, which 1759 puts at the heavy level: beside the other tests of the
machine the arenas of glibc warm up round by round for ten rounds and more. The scenario of local ports (1751 i)
is `tests/cli.rs` `a_client_out_of_local_ports`, run alone by a last step of the job on Linux with the range
narrowed to 100 ports (`SG_PORTS_NARROWED` set, so that it is never left out there unseen); its connections
past the ports give `AddressNotAvailable` (1764). The page is [What the servers stand](../../benchmarks/robustness.md), with the numbers of a
short try at 2% of the heavy level until the heavy level is run at night.

Found by the stand, and mended: SG's store answered every whole command in its block and kept the answers until
they were sent, so clients that ask for much and do not read held all they asked for, 3 GB for 20 of them (the C
store does the same and is measured as it is); a connection now holds 64 KB of answers and the last one whole.
`--idle-limit` covered only the wait for a request and was set again only while the block was empty: it never cut
a client that does not read and could cut a busy one whose block never emptied; it is now set again after each
whole request and covers the sending of the answers. Both servers take `--grace`, the HTTP server `--idle-limit`.
None of it changes the measured cases of the server lane. Found in the others: C's servers spin with their
descriptors taken (the listener without `EPOLLET`), `tokio`'s store holds more memory idle after each round of
resets and of clients that do not read, and hyper lets connections go late after resets and does not answer what
came before a closed sending half. Not understood: on macOS some of SG's sockets stay open in the kernel after
the client reset them, when many are reset at once (none of C's; never on Linux).

## 161. A slice that may be missing, and a linked source

HTTP was to come next (1765 to 1773, approved: `std/http`, a server of HTTP/1.1 first). A first try of its shape
found two things the language did not have, which every library that lends what it finds or sets a time limit of
its own needs, and they come first.

Approved on 23 November 2026, before the slice:

1774. **`read T[]?`, a slice that may be missing.** A function gives one back (`return null`, or a window onto
     what it was lent) and takes one; a struct that carries a borrow holds one; `is null`, `is not null`, which
     narrows it to the window, and `??` look into it; a list of windows gives one from `get` and `removeLast`
     (closed in 1732 for want of it). Only `read`: no `ref T[]?` until a program needs one. In C it is the
     window, 16 bytes, of length -1 when it is missing, as a `read string?` is; nothing is copied. Rust's
     `Option<&[u8]>`, Zig's `?[]const u8`.
1775. **`CancellationSource.linked(read token)`**: a source cancelled when the token's source is (at once when
     it already is), and by its own `cancel` and `cancelAfter`; cancelling it leaves the token's source as it
     is; linked to `CancellationToken.None` it is a plain source. One parent, as tokio's `child_token()` and
     Go's `context.WithCancel(parent)`: two tokens are joined by a chain. A source that is not linked pays
     nothing for it; a linked one is a place on its parent's list, let go under the clock's lock.

Built: the checker takes `read T[]?` and refuses `ref T[]?` and `T[]?` alone with a hint; compiled code lends it
as a window of length -1 for nothing, as text (`lent_none`, `lent_holds`). A linked source in C holds its parent
and sits on its list as a delay whose end cancels it; a source cancelled with its parent cancels the sources
linked to it before it ends its own waits, in the interpreter too. A linked source lets its parent go as soon as
it is cancelled, by its parent, by itself or by its clock: one that the clock still held for a later time kept
its cancelled parent alive until the end of the program, which the count of blocks showed. Tests:
`ok_lent_slice_option`, `error_slice_option_ref`, `error_slice_option_owned`, `ok_cancel_linked`,
`error_cancel_linked_name`; `error_list_of_borrows_window_get` is gone with what it refused.
