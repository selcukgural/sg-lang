## 198. A program at a terminal of its own, a terminal made raw, and its size

Choices 2027 and 2028 were approved with 196 ([26 November](2026-11-26.md#slice-196)): a pseudo terminal for a
started program, and the raw mode of a terminal tested through it. The details were proposed and approved on 27
November 2026:

2042. **Its ends are kept by the `Process`** (in place of the separate `PseudoTerminal` of 2027):
     `Process.takeTerminalReader()` → `StreamReader?`, `takeTerminalWriter()` → `StreamWriter?`, and
     `resizeTerminal(int rows, int columns)` → `Result<void, IoError>`. The pseudo console of Windows keeps the
     pipe of what it shows open until it is closed (`ClosePseudoConsole`); held by the `Process`, it is closed
     when the program has ended, which a separate value taken out of it could not promise.
2043. **`Command.terminal(int rows, int columns)`**: every stream not sent elsewhere goes to the terminal; one
     sent elsewhere (`outputTo`, `pipeError` and the others) stays there on Linux and macOS. On Windows, whose
     pseudo console takes all three streams or none, a stream sent elsewhere as well is `InvalidInput` where it
     starts.
2044. **A session of its own**, whose controlling terminal this is: the byte 3 written is `SIGINT` to the group in
     front at it, and job control works inside. `groupId` and `signalGroup` reach its group. With `foreground` a
     panic where it starts (as `foreground` with `newSession`, 197); with `newSession` or `newGroup` nothing more;
     in a `Pipeline` a panic.
2045. **Started with `fork` and `exec` on Linux and macOS** (the child of 2034 again): `TIOCSCTTY` makes the
     terminal that of the session, which `posix_spawn` cannot ask for on macOS; one way for both. The interpreter
     does it in `pre_exec`.
2046. **Only `start`**: `run`, `output`, `status` and their async forms of a program at a terminal of its own are
     a panic, since no one would read the terminal. What `output` would read through one (as `script` does) is in
     what is not here yet.
2047. **The end of its reader, and its bytes**: the reader ends once the program, and those it started that still
     hold the terminal, have ended; Linux says so with `EIO`, which the reader takes as its end. A `"\n"` the
     program writes comes as `"\r\n"`; what is written to the writer is shown back unless the program turned that
     off. The pseudo console of Windows writes sequences of its own among what the program wrote, so its bytes are
     not those of Linux and macOS. A terminal holds little: what the program writes is read while it runs.
2048. **`resizeTerminal`**: `TIOCSWINSZ`, and the program gets `SIGWINCH`; on Windows `ResizePseudoConsole`. Rows or
     columns outside 1 to 65535 are `InvalidInput`; after the end, nothing.
2049. **`Terminal.rawMode()`** → `Result<RawMode, IoError>`, of the input of this program (descriptor 0;
     `NotATerminal` when it is not one), as `cfmakeraw` makes it: no echo, no wait for a line, Ctrl-C and Ctrl-Z
     as the bytes 3 and 26, and `"\n"` not turned into `"\r\n"`. `RawMode : Disposable`, whose `restore(this)`
     puts the terminal back, as its end does. On Windows `SetConsoleMode`: the input without line input, echo and
     processed input, with the sequences of a terminal (`ENABLE_VIRTUAL_TERMINAL_INPUT`); the output with
     `ENABLE_VIRTUAL_TERMINAL_PROCESSING` and `DISABLE_NEWLINE_AUTO_RETURN`.
2050. **Put back by the language**: the end of the program, a panic, and a signal that would end it with no
     listener (`SIGHUP`, `SIGINT`, `SIGQUIT`, `SIGTERM`) put back the settings seen at the first `rawMode`.
2051. **`Terminal.size()`** → `Result<TerminalSize, IoError>`, new: the rows and columns of the terminal the output
     of this program goes to (`TIOCGWINSZ`; the window of the console on Windows); `NotATerminal` when it is not
     one.
2052. **Async**: the reader and the writer of a terminal are those of a pipe. No API of keys: `Stdin` reads them,
     and the sequences a key sends are the program's to read.
2053. **Tests and a measurement**: the golden programs below, a test of the command for the terminal put back, ten
     megabytes through a terminal, and the speed of a terminal against C (`posix_openpt` and a loop of `read`),
     and of a start at one, small, medium and large.

What was built. `Command` keeps the rows and the columns and gives them to `System.start` as `ways[8]` and
`ways[9]`, with way 7 (the terminal) for each stream not sent elsewhere and a session of its own on Linux and
macOS; `made` grew to eight numbers: the end read, the end written, and what names the terminal to two new calls,
`System.resizeTerminal` and `System.closeTerminal`. Three more serve `Terminal`: `System.terminalRaw`,
`System.terminalRestore` (the settings travel as sixteen numbers, which hold a `struct termios` of Linux or macOS,
or the two modes of a console) and `System.terminalSize`. Linux and macOS open the pseudo terminal with
`posix_openpt`, set its size, and start the program through the child of 2034, which makes it the terminal of the
session after `setsid`; the end the program got is closed in the parent, so the reader comes to its end with the
program. The end kept is read, written and resized through descriptors of their own; `write` waits for room
where another made the descriptor non-blocking (the reader of a terminal shares it with the writer). On Windows the
pseudo console is found in `kernel32.dll` when first asked for, so a program still starts on a Windows without
it; the program gets handles that name nothing for its three streams, or it would get those of this program
where they were sent elsewhere and write there; the pool of threads of the system closes the console as soon as
the program ends, so that the reader comes to its end without waiting for `Process.wait`, under a lock that
`resizeTerminal` takes too. The interpreter on Windows starts such a program with `CreateProcessW` itself, since
std's `Command` cannot give it a pseudo console; the command line is quoted as compiled code quotes it. A
`Process` lets go of the ends of its terminal only when it ends, not at `wait`: the pseudo console takes the end
of its keys as its own end, and would end the program.

Decided on the way, in the spirit of the choices. On Windows a console tells a program of a new size among the
events of its input, not as a signal: there the program gets no `Signal.WindowChange`, and the pseudo console
draws its whole screen again. Ctrl-D ends the input only on Linux and macOS. Closing the writer on Windows ends
the program. A program at a terminal of its own is in no group of its own on Windows (`groupId` is `null`).
`Stdin.isTerminal()` in an async function was taken for a read that holds the thread (since 182); asking whether
a stream is a terminal waits for nothing, and is allowed there now.

Found and left: in the interpreter, an async read of the pipe of another program, and so of its terminal, holds
the run while it waits, so a task that reads while another writes to the same program waits for ever; compiled
code waits on the loop of the network. The tests write first and read after. Kept as [issue
10](https://github.com/selcukgural/sg-lang/issues/10).

Fixed the same day, after the slice, with no choice to make, since it changes no rule: the interpreter reads the
end of a pipe of another program, and of its terminal, as it reads its own input. An async read that has to wait
gives a thread one read of the system into the block of the reader while the task waits on the loop of the run,
and a cancel ends the wait without losing what the thread reads. An async write whose block has to go out to
such a pipe writes it on a thread while the task waits, as compiled code writes it on a thread of input and
output; that wait is not cut short by a token, which is looked at before. The same way on Linux, macOS and
Windows, where a pipe that is not a socket cannot be waited for by the loop. Before, the issue's program waited
for ever; now it ends interpreted in under a second, as compiled. Tested by `ok_process_pipes_both_ways` (up to
10 MB through `sg-test-tool cat` while it is read, a write of 1 MB at once, a line sent and read back a thousand
times, `readBytesAsync`, `fillAsync` and the standard error beside the output, a read cut short by its token
that loses nothing, writing to a program that has ended) and `ok_process_terminal_both_ways` (50,000 keys into
a raw terminal while what it shows is read), both of which waited for ever interpreted before the fix, the same
on all three systems. On the way: `sg-test-tool raw=N` ends its lines with `\r\n`, since a raw terminal does
not go back to the start of the line, and on Windows the pseudo console drew the lines stepping to the right
until one was cut where the line wrapped.

**Measured** (release builds; median of seven runs; the program at the terminal is the system's `head -c N
/dev/zero` for the reads and `true` for the starts; C opens the terminal itself, forks, `setsid`, `TIOCSCTTY`,
`exec`, and reads in blocks of 64 KB as SG does):

macOS, M-series:

| Case | C | SG | SG / C |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 MB through a terminal | 14.3 ms | 13.9 ms | 0.97 |
| 64 MB | 634.5 ms | 608.4 ms | 0.96 |
| 512 MB | 5,025.3 ms | 4,917.1 ms | 0.98 |
| `true` at a terminal of its own, 100 times | 192.6 ms | 169.0 ms | 0.88 |
| the same, 1,000 times | 1,926.5 ms | 1,718.7 ms | 0.89 |
| the same, 5,000 times | 9,897.7 ms | 8,982.0 ms | 0.91 |

|---|---|---|---|

A terminal carries what it carries: SG reads as fast as C. A start at one is 15% slower than C on Linux, about 33
µs each: SG's child is the careful one of 2034 (a pipe that tells the parent why `exec` failed, every signal held
off across the `fork`, the signals of a terminal set back), where the C of the measurement does none of that. Its
list of open descriptors, read from `/proc/self/fd` before each `fork` for when `close_range` is missing, is now
read only where `close_range` does not work (asked once), which took 2% off. Written in [the slower
places](../../benchmarks/slower-than-c.md).

After the slice, approved on 27 November 2026 as the measurement above proposed it:

2054. **On Linux a program at a terminal of its own is started by `posix_spawn`**, as one without is (in place of
     `fork` and `exec` of 2045 there; macOS keeps them). `POSIX_SPAWN_SETSID` makes the session first, and the
     first stream that goes to the terminal opens it by its name in that session without `O_NOCTTY`, which makes
     it the controlling terminal as `TIOCSCTTY` does; the other streams at it are copies of that one. The three
     signals of a terminal (`SIGTSTP`, `SIGTTIN`, `SIGTTOU`) get their default, as the child of 2034 gives them.
     The end of the terminal opened here stays open until `posix_spawn` has returned, which is after the program
     runs, so the reader never sees the end before the program has opened it. A C library without
     `POSIX_SPAWN_SETSID` (glibc before 2.26) keeps the `fork`. The interpreter is as it was (std's `Command`
     forks once it is given `pre_exec`).

**Measured** (Linux, aarch64 in a container on the same machine, release builds, median of nine runs, the four
programs run in turn; `true` at a terminal of its own, started and waited for; SG before is 5fc5551; C is the
program above with `fork`, and the same with `posix_spawn` as SG now starts it):

| Starts | C, `fork` | C, `posix_spawn` | SG before | SG | SG / SG before | SG / C `posix_spawn` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 21.6 ms | 18.8 ms | 25.1 ms | 20.0 ms | 0.80 | 1.06 |
| 1,000 | 220.5 ms | 190.9 ms | 263.2 ms | 195.4 ms | 0.74 | 1.02 |
| 5,000 | 1,109.8 ms | 939.6 ms | 1,256.0 ms | 991.5 ms | 0.79 | 1.06 |

A start at a terminal of its own is a fifth faster than before and 0.89 times C with `fork`; against C started
the same way it is 2 to 6% slower, up to about 10 µs a start; not looked into yet, the likely cause is what SG

two more descriptors of the end kept). The seven
golden programs of the terminal and those of groups, sessions and job control give the same output on Linux.

**Tests**: `ok_process_terminal` (what the program sees, its output as the terminal turns it, keys shown back and

## The writers of the standard streams into a pipe no one reads (issue 7)

[Issue 7](https://github.com/selcukgural/sg-lang/issues/7), found in 196: a compiled program that writes with
`Stdout.writeAsync` into a pipe whose reader has gone ended now by `SIGPIPE` and now with the error `connection
reset`. The cause: macOS gives the signal only after the write has returned `EPIPE`, as it does to C's own programs;
the error came back to `main`, whose end leaves the signal out (1603), and the signal was lost when it came after
that. On macOS 59 runs of 60 ended with the error. The interpreter ended `Stdout`'s by the signal every time, and
gave the error for `Stderr`'s. Proposed and approved on 27 November 2026:

2055. **A writer of `Stdout` or `Stderr` into a pipe whose reader is gone ends the program as `print` and
     `printError` do** (1601), every time, interpreted and compiled, async or not: by `SIGPIPE` on Linux and macOS,
     set back to its default first, so also when the program was started with the signal ignored. On Windows,
     which has no such signal, `Stdout` ends the program with 0, as `print` does, and `Stderr` gives the error, as
     the interpreter's did (`printError` there still loses the line and goes on). In the place of: always the
     error, which would have set the writers apart from `print`.

Built the same day. Compiled code ends the program where the write of a standard stream gives `EPIPE`
(`sg_std_ended`), before the error can reach `main`; the interpreter's `Stderr` ends it as its `printError` does. On
Windows C's `stderr` takes what a pipe whose reader is gone refuses and says it was written, so a writer of a
`Stderr` that is not a console writes with `WriteFile` itself, and gets the error. Tested by
`ok_process_pipe_closed_writer` (`Stdout.writeAsync`, `Stdout.write` and `Stderr.writeAsync`, each five times into
`sg-test-tool head=6`, every run ending one way; `.windows.out` for Windows) and the writer of `Stderr` in
`an_error_output_closed_early` (cli), started with the signal ignored too. The program of the issue ended by the
signal 30 times of 30 on macOS. `sg-test-tool` has the steps `ywrite`, `ysync` and `yerror` for it. Once, under the
load of the full tests, the old build ended with the signal 10 (`SIGBUS`) on macOS; it did not come again in 90
runs before the change or after it.

## 199. Watching a folder

Proposed and approved on 27 November 2026, the next of the gaps of a language of systems (the plan of 26 November):

2056. **`Directory.watch(path)`** → `Result<DirectoryWatcher, IoError>`; `DirectoryWatcher : Disposable`, with
     `next(ref this)` → `Result<FileChange, IoError>` and `nextAsync(ref this, token)` → the same (a cancel is
     `IoError.Cancelled`). The shape of `SignalListener`.
2057. **`Directory.watchAll(path)`** watches the whole tree, as `createAll` and `deleteAll` reach it; `watch` only what
     is in the folder. On Linux a watch of inotify for each folder; a folder made later is watched and read at once,
     and what is found in it is told as made (the gap between a folder made and its watch closed); a link is not
     followed.
2058. **`public enum FileChange { Created(string path), Deleted(string path), Changed(string path), Lost(string
     path) }`**: the path is the folder as given joined with the name (`Path.join(folder, name)`), a name that is not
     UTF-8 escaped as 189 escapes it. A rename is the old name deleted and the new one made, on every system: FSEvents
     cannot pair them. `Changed` is a file written or its attributes changed. A folder's own change because what is
     in it changed is not told, as on Linux. `Lost`: the system's queue ran over (inotify's `IN_Q_OVERFLOW`, a block
     of Windows too small, FSEvents' `MustScanSubDirs` and the events it dropped); the program lists that folder
     again.
2059. **The folder itself gone** (deleted or moved away): what came before, then `next` gives `NotFound` for ever. A
     file given is `NotDirectory`. No room left for a watch of Linux (`max_user_watches`): while `watchAll` starts,
     the error of the call; for a folder made later, a `Lost` of it.
2060. **The systems**: inotify on Linux; FSEvents on macOS (CoreServices, an event of each file, linked only into a
     program that watches; the kind decided from the flags and whether the name is there; `watch` keeps what is
     directly in the folder); `ReadDirectoryChangesW` on Windows, with a block of 64 KB and the whole tree for
     `watchAll`.
2061. **Twice the same, and a limit**: the same change twice in a row before the program takes it is one; at most
     16,384 changes wait, and beyond that they are dropped and one `Lost` of the folder says so. No waiting for a
     quiet time (debounce) in `std`: a program does it with its own timers.
2062. **From when the call returns**: what is there already is not told; every change after the call returns is.
2063. **The interpreter the same**, by hand-written calls of the systems in Rust (no crates), its output that of
     compiled code.
2064. **Measured** against the programs of C that do this: `inotifywait -m -r` on Linux, `fswatch` on macOS: the delay
     from a file made to its line, bursts of 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000 files, and the start, memory and descriptors of
     `watchAll` on trees of 1,000 and 10,000 folders.
2065. **Tests** that push it: more changes than it keeps, a deep tree made while it watches, a folder moved in and out,
     the folder itself deleted, names that are not UTF-8, a link back to the folder, a cancel, the same output on the
     three systems.

What was built. `Directory.watch`, `watchAll` and their async forms (`watchAsync`, `watchAllAsync`, which find the
folders under it on a thread of input and output, as the other calls of `Directory` do) start a watcher of the system
through `System.watchStart(path, all)`; a `DirectoryWatcher` takes all the changes kept at once
(`System.watchNext(watcher, block, ref names, ref kinds)`) and gives them one by one; `nextAsync` waits with
`System.watchWaitAsync` until there is one or the folder is gone, as `SignalListener` waits. Each watcher has a queue
filled by the side of the system: on Linux a thread of its own that reads inotify and keeps a table from each watch
to its folder; on macOS FSEvents on a dispatch queue of its own; on Windows a thread of its own that waits for
`ReadDirectoryChangesExW`, which says of each name whether it is a folder. The interpreter (`src/watch.rs`) does the
same, the same way; on macOS it takes CoreServices by name when the first watch starts, so that a run that watches
nothing loads nothing more.

Decided on the way, in the spirit of the choices:

- **A thread of each watcher** waits on the system (inotify, `ReadDirectoryChangesExW`), not the loop of the network
  (2060 said so of Linux and Windows): `next` and `nextAsync` then take from one queue, and the system's queue is
  emptied while the program does something else, which keeps `Lost` rare. FSEvents has its own dispatch queue.
- **A folder is never `Changed`** on any system (2058 dropped only a folder changed because of what is in it): Windows
  cannot tell a folder's attributes changed from what is in it changed, so Linux's change of a folder's attributes
  is not told either.
- **macOS keeps the names it knows.** FSEvents gathers the flags of a file over several seconds, from before the
  watch too: a file made before `watch` and written after came with `Created` and `Changed`, for up to 12 seconds in
  a measurement, and changes made just before the call came after it. So the watcher learns what is in the folder
  (`watch`) or the tree (`watchAll`) before the stream starts, each name with its node, and tells a change by them
  and the disk: a name it does not know that is there is made, a name it knows that is gone is deleted, another
  node at a known name is the old one deleted and a new one made, and only "written" is taken from the flags. A
  folder deleted takes the names under it out; a `Lost` learns the folder again. It costs memory, about 65 bytes
  a name, and the start reads the tree (below). A file made and deleted before FSEvents tells of it is not told.
- **On Windows the watch starts once the first call is made**: Windows keeps changes only from the first
  `ReadDirectoryChangesExW`, so `watch` waits until the thread has made it (2062). A link made as a folder is told
  before it is a link, so what a folder that came in is when it is read decides whether it is gone into. A folder
  moved away keeps its handle, and is found gone after the next change in it (its final path is another).
- **A token cancelled already takes nothing**, as with `SignalListener`, even when a change is kept.
- On Linux and Windows a file made just as its folder comes in may be told as made twice: once found when the folder
  is read, once by the system. Untaken one after the other they are one; a program that took the first between sees
  both. On macOS the names it knows tell it once.

**Measured** (release builds; the watcher is a program of its own that writes a line for each change, read by a

its thread as well. On Linux SG is as quick as inotifywait or
quicker and keeps less. On macOS the delay is FSEvents' and the same; the names SG keeps cost about 6.5 MB at 100,000
and a start that reads the tree, 274 ms at 110,000 names, which fswatch does not pay and for which it tells files
made before it started as made. The first measurement of SG on macOS kept 10.2 MB with nothing watched: CoreServices
loaded by `dlopen` when the first watch starts kept 7.7 MB more than the same library linked into the program (3.9
MB), so compiled code links it, as 2060 said (6.0 MB now), and only the interpreter takes it by name.

**Tests**: `ok_watch` (nothing told of what was there or changed before, a file written, made and deleted deep, a
rename, a folder moved in with what is in it and written in after, the folder moved away, a folder's permissions not
told, a cancel that loses nothing and a token cancelled already, `watch` not telling what is in a folder in it, a file
`NotDirectory`, a missing folder `NotFound`, the folder deleted: `NotFound` and again); `ok_watch_lost` (20,000
folders made with nothing taken: a `Lost` of the folder, the changes read until the watcher is quiet, the folder
listed again, changes after it, each found as made or, after a `Lost`, by a listing (macOS under load drops events
and says so); 200 writes before one is taken are fewer changes than writes; every wait has a limit, since what is
dropped may be the very change a program waits for); `ok_watch_deep` (a change at the bottom of 200 folders
watched; 200 folders made one in another while it watches and a file in the last, all told, or a `Lost` and what is
there listed: Windows' block of 64 KB holds fewer names that long); `ok_watch_names` (`next` holding the thread, a
name with letters that are not ASCII, one that is not UTF-8 where the system keeps it, each name as `Directory.list`
gives it, a link to the folder itself not gone into); `error_watch_next_in_async`, `error_watch_in_async`. A phase of
a test ends at a folder made, which is never changed, and does not show a change of a name it shows made: Linux and
Windows tell a file made and written as both, macOS as made. The output is the same on macOS, Linux and Windows,
interpreted and compiled.

<a id="slice-200"></a>
## 200. A folder as a root, a file read and written through one opening, a file with no name

Proposed and approved on 27 November 2026, the next of the gaps of a language of systems (the plan of 26 November):
what C has as `openat`, `"r+"` and `tmpfile`, and Go as `os.Root`.

Until now a name that came from outside the program was checked as a text (`Path.joinInside`, 954) and then opened
as any path is: a link inside the folder that pointed out of it was followed. A writer of a file was opened only to
be written, so the same file was read by opening its path a second time, which a file with no name does not have.
And `File.createTemp()` gives a file with a name, which the program removes.

A folder as a root:

2066. **`Directory.openRoot(read string path)`** → `Result<DirectoryRoot, IoError>`; `DirectoryRoot : Disposable`
     holds the handle of the folder, not its path: a folder renamed or moved is still the root, and a folder put
     where it was is another. Several tasks may use one root.
2067. **A link is followed while it stays inside.** A path that leads out is refused: one that climbs past the folder
     with `..`, one that starts at the root of the system, and one that goes through a link that does either (a link
     whose text starts at the root of the system is refused, wherever it points). Go's rule for `os.Root`. The other
     way, refusing every link, was not taken: a tree with `current -> releases/v2` in it would not open.
2068. **`IoError.OutsideRoot`** is what such a path gives, a variant of its own, so that a server can tell it (a 403)
     from a name that is not there. A `match` over every variant of `IoError` names it from now on.
2069. **The first calls**: `openText`, `openRegular`, `createText`, `openWrite`, `openAppend`, `info`, `linkInfo`,
     `exists`, `list`, `createDirectory`, `delete`, `deleteDirectory`, and `openRoot` for a folder inside as a root of
     its own; each with an `Async` form. Moving, copying, links, permissions, `deleteAll`, `createAll` and the calls
     that read or write a whole file are not there yet.
2070. **Names.** What `Path.joinInside` refuses is `InvalidInput` under a root on every system: an empty path or an
     empty part (`a//b`, `a/`), a `\` or a `:`, a control character, a part that ends with `.` or a space, the name
     of a device of Windows. The difference: `.` and `..` that stay inside are parts as any other.
2071. **How it is found.** On Linux the kernel is asked to keep the path under the folder (`openat2` with
     `RESOLVE_BENEATH`), one call; where it has no such call, and on macOS and Windows, the path is walked part by
     part from the handle of the root, a link read and not followed, at most 40 links. The interpreter the same.
2072. **Measured**, SG against SG: a file opened by `Path.joinInside` and `File.openRegular`, and through a root, at
     depths of 1, 4 and 16, on Linux and macOS; and the file server of the server lane with and without a root.

One file read and written through one opening:

2073. **`File.openUpdate(read string path)`** → `Result<StreamWriter, IoError>`: the file is opened to be read and
     written. It must be there (`NotFound`), is not emptied, and is written from its start.
2074. **`writer.openReader()`** → `Result<StreamReader, IoError>`: a reader of the same open file, with a place of its
     own, for a writer of `File.openUpdate` or `File.openTemp`; of any other writer, `InvalidInput`. The rule is that
     of a stream of C opened with `"r+"`: the reader sees what the writer has flushed, and what its block holds stays
     until it seeks. No new type that reads and writes (1213 stands); a file with `readAt` and `writeAt` was not taken.
2075. **`reader.position()` and `writer.position()`** → `Result<long, IoError>`: where the next read or write starts,
     without moving the stream and without writing what a writer holds.

A file with no name:

2076. **`File.openTemp()`** (and `openTempAsync`) → `Result<StreamWriter, IoError>`: a new file in `Directory.temp()`
     that has no name, opened to be read too (`openReader`), and gone when its last stream is closed, also when the
     program fails or is ended from outside. `O_TMPFILE` on Linux (where the file system has none: made as `mkstemp`
     makes it and its name taken away at once, as on macOS); on Windows `FILE_FLAG_DELETE_ON_CLOSE`, where the file
     has a name in the folder while it is open.
2077. **Tests** that push it: `..`, a path from the root of the system, links out, a chain of 41 links, a ring, the
     root renamed while it is open, a thousand folders one in another; a hundred thousand calls while another program
     swaps the names asked for between what is inside and links that lead out; a gigabyte through a file with no
     name; no file left when the program is ended while it holds one; a file written and read at places a seed
     gives, compared byte by byte with a program of C that uses `"r+"`.

What was built. `DirectoryRoot` is written in SG (`std/files.sg`): each call checks the name (2070) and hands the
handle of the root and the path to a call of the system (`System.rootOpen`, `rootReader`, `rootWriter`, `rootStatus`,
`rootList`, `rootMake`, `rootRemove`, `rootClose`), which gives the readers and writers `File` gives. Compiled code
does the work in `ROOT_RUNTIME`, the interpreter in `src/root.rs` (Linux and macOS) and `src/sys/root_windows.rs`.

- **The walk** (`sg_root_reach`) goes from the handle of the root: each folder on the way is opened by its name in
  the folder before it, a link not followed; a link is read and its parts put in front of what is left of the
  path; what it gives is the folder that holds the last part and the name of that part, and the call is made on
  the name without following a link. A name changed to a link between the walk and the call is an error that is
  walked again a few times, never a way out.
- **`..` is one step, however deep the walk is.** On Linux and macOS the system is asked what is above the folder
  at hand, and the answer is taken only when it is the very folder the walk went into before (the same device and
  file number, kept for a path with a `..` in it); when a folder was moved in between it is not, and the names are
  walked again from the root. On Windows the folders gone into stay open until the walk ends, and `..` goes back
  to the one before. The first form walked from the root for every `..`: a path of a thousand folders down and a
  thousand `..` cost half a million calls of the system, 20 seconds on macOS, which a client of a server could ask
  for; it is now linear.
- **Where the system keeps a path under a folder itself**, it is asked first: `openat2` with `RESOLVE_BENEATH` on
  Linux (with `RESOLVE_CACHED` too in an async call tried on the task's own thread, 1872); on macOS `O_NOFOLLOW_ANY`
  and `AT_SYMLINK_NOFOLLOW_ANY`, which refuse every link, for a path without `..`, and the walk when a link is met.
  A kernel without the call, a filter of system calls that refuses it, and a path longer than the system takes in
  one piece go to the walk. A program built with `-DSG_ROOT_WALK_ONLY` always walks: the tests run both.
- **A path longer than the system's limit works under a root**, since it is walked part by part: a file two
  thousand bytes down is made, read and removed on macOS, whose limit is 1,024.
- **On Windows** each part is opened with `NtCreateFile` and the handle of the folder before it, without following a
  point that stands for another name. A symbolic link is read and walked on when it says where it leads from its
  own folder; one that names a place from a drive, and a junction, which always does, are `OutsideRoot`. A point that
  only marks a file kept by a service is opened as the system opens it.
- **A reader of `openReader` keeps its place itself** (`placed`): on Linux and macOS it is one more descriptor of the
  open file of its writer, which shares where the file stands, so it reads with `pread` at its own place; on
  Windows the file is opened once more through its handle (`ReOpenFile`) and read at a place the same way. A seek of
  such a reader always lets its block go, also a seek to where it stands (`SeekFrom.Current(0)`), so that it reads
  again what its writer has written since.
- **`position()`** of a reader is where its file stands less what its block holds, of a writer where its file stands
  and what it holds beyond; `Stdin`, `Stdout`, `Stderr` and the reader of a connection have none (a compile error,
  as for `seek`), and a pipe and a writer of `openAppend` are `NotSeekable`.

Decided on the way, in the spirit of the choices:

- **`exists(path)` under a root** is whether the path names anything inside, a file or a folder, a link followed:
  the root has no `File` and `Directory` to ask separately.
- **`delete` removes a file or the link itself**, and `deleteDirectory` an empty folder; a link to a folder is no
  folder to `deleteDirectory` (`NotDirectory`) on any system. The root is not removed through itself
  (`InvalidInput`). `linkInfo` and `delete` do not follow the last part, so a link that leads out can be looked at
  and removed.
- **A ring of links, and more than 40 in a row**, is `IoError.Other` with the system's code for it, as elsewhere.
- **The file server of the server lane keeps following links**, as nginx and its other three versions do, and as
  its check holds them to. It got an option (`--inside 1`) that opens its files through a root, measured beside the
  four as `sg-root`; a link out is then a 403.
- **On Windows a folder that holds an open file or folder is not renamed**, by anyone; the root's own handle does not
  stop a rename. And a link made to something that is not there yet is a link to a file, which Windows does not go
  through as a folder (`NotDirectory`), where Linux and macOS follow it.

Two errors the tests found on the way, both mended before anything was pushed: a reader with a place of its own read
the same place twice inside one `readBytes` that met the end of the file (the place was counted after the read, not
as it went), found by the comparison with C; and `info` under a root told a link as itself when the name was swapped
for a link just after the walk had found it none, found by the swapping test.

## The rule of speed: held to safe Rust, measured against C

Proposed and approved on 27 November 2026:

2078. **What SG is held to in speed is safe Rust doing the same work: SG is not to be slower. C is the yardstick, not
     the rival.** Until now the rule was read as "not slower than C". But C leaves memory to the programmer and pays
     nothing while the program runs; SG keeps memory safe itself, and where that needs a check at run time (the
     bounds of a list, the count of a `shared` value, two windows of one list held apart, 194), C does not pay and
     safe Rust does. Against C the rule cannot hold at every such place; against the language that gives the same
     guarantee it can. C stays what SG is measured against wherever a program of C exists: SG compiles through C, so
     C written by hand is the ceiling, and the distance to it is what safety costs. A place where SG is slower than
     C is the price of the guarantee where safe Rust pays the same, and ours to mend (the code SG writes, or its
     library) where it does not. Every entry of [Slower than C](../../benchmarks/slower-than-c.md) says which, as
     it is next looked at; nothing is measured again for it.

What stays as it was: what is new is not written again in Rust to have a rival (25 November); where a mature
program of Rust does the job it is the rival in the table, and where the program that exists is C, as for a port,
SG is measured against that and the cause of a difference is written down; a case SG loses, against Rust as against
C, is published as plainly as one it wins (1554).

Written into [the idea of the language](../04-decisions-and-open-questions.md), [How it is
measured](../../benchmarks/method.md#the-rival-and-the-yardstick), the front page of the benchmarks and the top of
Slower than C, whose two newest entries have their **Why**.

## The sentence and the tagline

Proposed and approved on 27 November 2026, after the rule of speed:

2079. **The one sentence of SG is "a systems language: small like C, readable like C#, fast and memory safe like
     Rust", and its tagline is "Plain to read. Fast to run. Memory safe by design."** The sentence said "as fast as
     C" and the front pages had a part called "As fast as C"; by 2078 that is not what SG is held to, and it is not
     what the numbers say at every place. So each language is named for what SG takes from it: from C that it is
     small, from C# how it reads, from Rust both its speed and its safety, the two things 2078 holds SG to against
     Rust. "Like" is written, not "as fast as": a case SG loses is published (1554), and the sentence is not to
     claim more than the tables do. The tagline was "Safe by design. Fast by default." (1405); it now names the
     three things in the order a reader meets them: the code is plain to read, the program is fast, and memory is
     safe because the language is made so. "Plain to read" is said, not "easy to write": the promise is software
     that is safe and fast, not fast development (12 November). The part of the front pages is now called "Fast to
     run" and says what SG is held to; the numbers under it are the same.

Written into the root README, the front page of the site and its tagline, [Why SG](../../website/docs/about/why.md),
[the idea of the language](../04-decisions-and-open-questions.md), the front page of the benchmarks, `funding.json`
and the README of the brand. Not changed: the social card, whose picture carries the tagline before this one as
outlines; it is drawn again with the next logo. What was written on earlier days stays as it was written.

## The tagline, again: no other language named

Proposed and approved on 27 November 2026, the same day as 2079, which it replaces:

2080. **The tagline is "Small enough to know by heart. Safe and fast by construction.", and the one sentence of SG
     is "a small systems language that makes fast software safe by construction". The line "Plain to read. Fast to
     run. Memory safe by design." is dropped, and no other language is named in either.** 2079 named C, C# and Rust
     for what SG takes from each; read on a front page, that made SG sound like a mix of others rather than a
     language with its own point. The new tagline says the two things that set SG apart, in the order a reader
     meets them. "Small enough to know by heart" is what Rust, which is also safe and fast, does not offer: the
     whole language fits in one head. "Safe and fast by construction" says where both come from: safety from what
     the compiler accepts, not from a check while the program runs, and speed from having no collector and no
     runtime to carry; one phrase holds both claims, and it claims no more than the tables show ("as fast as C"
     is not said). It holds outside `unsafe`, which the book and the rules set apart, as Rust's own promise does.
     The front pages had the old line above the sentence; with the tagline itself as the heading, the two would
     say the same thing twice, so the line goes. The parts of the front pages are now called Small, Fast and Safe
     (the plain site) or keep their names with "Fast to run" made "Fast" (README and the current site).

Written into the root README, the front page of the site and its tagline, [Why SG](../../website/docs/about/why.md),
[the idea of the language](../04-decisions-and-open-questions.md), `funding.json` and the README of the brand.
Not changed: the social card (as under 2079), and the description of the repository on GitHub, which is set outside
the repository.
