## 153. Time, random numbers and the log measured against Rust and Zig

Choices made in the hundred-and-fifty-third slice (time, random numbers and the log measured against Rust and
Zig). 1667 to 1671 were approved at its start, 1672 while the trials were written, 1673 to 1677 after the
profile of the first measurement, between 21 and 22 November 2026:

1667. **The rivals.** For `std/time`, Rust's `chrono` 0.4.45; Zig's library reads the clocks but has no
     calendar before 1970, no ISO 8601 and no time zones, so its program writes the calendar and the text
     by hand, with `std.fmt`, and asks the C library's `localtime_r` for the time zone, and the table says
     so. For `std/random`, Rust's `rand` 0.10 with `rand_xoshiro` 0.8's `Xoshiro256StarStar` from
     `seed_from_u64`, which is SG's generator, xoshiro256\*\* seeded with SplitMix64, and gives the same
     numbers; Zig's `std.Random.DefaultPrng` is xoshiro256++, other numbers, so nothing the programs print
     depends on the numbers drawn. For `std/log`, Rust's `log` 0.4 with `env_logger` 0.11, given the form of
     SG's lines; Zig's `std.log` with a `logFn` that writes the same lines, its own having no time.
1668. **The cases of time**, chosen before the numbers were seen: ten million moments between 1900 and
     2200, a quarter of them whole seconds and the others with milliseconds, microseconds or nanoseconds,
     each written in ISO 8601 into a text kept from one to the next; 100 MB of moments in RFC 3339 with
     offsets and fractions of 0 to 9 digits, from a new maker, `trials/inputs/time.sg`, each read and taken
     to UTC; a million moments seen in the time zone of the system, `TZ=Europe/Istanbul`; the monotonic
     clock read ten million times.
1669. **The cases of random numbers**: a hundred million numbers of 64 bits, the bits set counted; a hundred
     million numbers up to 1,000 in a histogram; 100 MB of bytes, 64 KB at a time into one buffer; a
     million tokens of 64 hex digits from the random bytes of the system (Rust's `SysRng`, Zig's
     `Io.random`, which is a generator for keys kept in the process and seeded by the system). What is
     printed is whether the bits, each bar and the mean of the bytes lie within six standard deviations.
1670. **The cases of the log**, each line on the standard error written into a file: lines at the level
     Info; lines at Debug while the log writes from Info, none written and the text of none made; four
     tasks writing at once. `measure.py` holds every line to its form, a time to the millisecond, the level
     and the message, and the messages, in the order of their text, to be the same everywhere.
1671. **The rest as the frame has it** (1631, 1634, 1635): the tests run the trials small, a case more than
     1.10 times the faster of Rust and Zig is profiled, and the ways a program fails are not measured.
1672. **A million lines, not ten**, in the first and third cases of the log: each line is one write of the
     system in all three languages, ten million took 20 to 45 seconds a run, and a million (60 MB) show the
     same. The lines that are off stay a hundred million.
1673. **What was made faster, for every program, with the same output.**
     - A number written with a least count of digits (`{n:D2}`, `%.2lld` in C) is written digit by digit
       into the text, as one without was, not through `snprintf`: each part of a date went through the
       parsing of a format. The digits are counted first and each written once into the room of the text,
       the zeros before them by the same loop, with no `memcpy` or `memset` of a few bytes.
     - On macOS the lines of `printError` from several threads wait for each other on an `os_unfair_lock`
       of the program's own, not on the lock of C's stream, which waits in the kernel; the line is one
       `fwrite` as before.
     - `Random.fill` copies the eight bytes of each draw at once (`copyFrom`), and `SecureRandom.hexString`
       writes 64 digits at a time through `Hex.encodeInto`; the same bytes.

     A copy of a few bytes into a text with loads and stores of a fixed size, in place of `memcpy`, was tried
     for the reading of RFC 3339, gained nothing, and was taken out.
1674. **The monotonic clock on macOS is `CLOCK_UPTIME_RAW`**, read with `clock_gettime_nsec_np`, in compiled
     code and in the interpreter: it stops while the machine sleeps, as Linux's `CLOCK_MONOTONIC` and Rust's
     `Instant` do, where `CLOCK_MONOTONIC` of macOS went on. Ten million readings take 0.105 s in C where they
     took 0.184; the timers of tasks keep their clock.
1675. **A moment in the time zone stays with `localtime_r`**, written under "Slower than C": `chrono` reads the
     TZif file of the zone once and finds each offset itself, 3.9 times as fast; a reader of TZif of SG's own
     is a direction, Windows keeping the rules of its system.
1676. **The random bytes of the system stay the system's** (`arc4random_buf`, `getrandom`): Zig's generator
     kept in the process is 4.7 times as fast for a token, but a generator in the process has to know when the
     process is forked and keep its key out of a dump of memory; Rust's `SysRng`, which asks the system each
     time as SG does, takes four times SG's time.
1677. **Written as they are**: Zig removes a line that is off when the program is compiled, where SG reads the
     level as the program runs (and is faster than Rust); Zig's xoshiro256++ is cheaper than xoshiro256\*\*, and
     Rust's xoshiro256\*\* takes SG's time to within 1.10; reading RFC 3339 takes 1.10 times Zig's hand-written
     reader.

Before 1673 and 1674: 1.183 s to write ten million moments, 0.178 s for the clock, 0.031 s for 100 MB of
random bytes, 0.341 s for a million tokens and 4.209 s for the lines of four tasks; after, 0.490 s (Rust
0.497), 0.097 s (Rust 0.166, Zig 0.158), 0.014 s (as both), 0.242 s and 2.761 s (Rust 3.642, Zig 2.567).

Tested by `the_trial_of_time`, `the_trial_of_random` and `the_trial_of_log` (new): the SG programs,
interpreted and compiled, write 1,000 moments and read seven of RFC 3339 (before 1970, offsets of either sign,
fractions of one to nine digits) as `chrono` does, see 1,000 moments in Istanbul as `chrono` sees them where
the system has the zone, draw numbers that look random, and write lines of the log in their form, whole from
four tasks. That `Random` gives the numbers of `rand_xoshiro` was held, outside the tests, on a million numbers
and a `fill`; `ok_random_seeded` holds the first numbers of three seeds to the reference code.
The whole of the tests passes on macOS and on Linux, with 508 of the 1926 programs compiled through C and run
clean under the sanitizers, 109 of them under the thread sanitizer.

## 154. The network measured against Rust and Zig

Choices made in the hundred-and-fifty-fourth slice (the network measured against Rust and Zig). 1678 to 1682
were approved at its start, 1683 to 1686 after the measurement stopped for good one night, 1687 to 1690 after
the profile of the first measurement, on 22 November 2026:

1678. **The rivals.** Rust's `std::net` where the functions block, a thread for each task, and `tokio` 1.x with
     the runtime of several threads `#[tokio::main]` makes where they are async, the runtime Rust's servers
     use; Zig 0.17.1's `std.Io.net` on `Io.Threaded`, its default, whose async functions are threads too, since
     its loop on Dispatch has no network on macOS and the one on kqueue is not done; the table says so. Zig is
     built `ReleaseFast` and `ReleaseSafe`.
1679. **The cases of TCP**, chosen before the numbers were seen, the server and its clients tasks of one
     program over the loopback: 8 clients each sending 20,000 messages of 64 bytes, 4 KB and 64 KB and waiting
     for each to come back; 1,000 clients at once, 100 messages each (async alone); 50,000 short connections one
     after another, a byte there and back on each; 1 GB one way, in pieces of 4 KB and 64 KB, the receiver
     counting and adding; 100 MB of a log sent over a connection and read line by line (`readLineAsync`,
     `BufReader::read_line`, `takeDelimiter`). Each with async functions (SG, `tokio`, Zig) and with functions
     that block (SG, `std::net`).
1680. **The cases of UDP**: 200,000 datagrams there and back from 8 sockets, of 64 bytes, 1 KB and 8 KB, async
     and blocking.
1681. **The addresses**: ten million lines, half v4 and half v6 with their ports, from a new maker,
     `trials/inputs/address.sg`, a v6 address written in one of four ways (as RFC 5952 writes it, four digits
     to a group in lower or upper case, the first run of zeros as `::`) and never a v4 address inside a v6
     one, which languages write in other ways; each read (`SocketAddress.parse`, `SocketAddr::from_str`,
     `IpAddress.parseLiteral`) and written back into a text kept from one to the next. What each program
     writes of 1,000 of them into a file is held to what Python's `ipaddress` writes. A lookup of a name is
     not measured: it is the time of the system's `getaddrinfo`.
1682. **What is held**: counts and sums the programs print, the same everywhere; each listens on port 0 and
     its clients find the port; Linux only checks that the outputs are the same; the rest is the frame of
     1631, 1634 and 1635.
1683. **A run that hangs stops the measuring**: one that has not ended after five minutes has what its threads
     were doing (`sample` on macOS) and the files it holds (`lsof`) written into `trials/target/hung-PID.txt`,
     and is killed. One night SG's program of 50,000 connections waited for nine hours: the clients had asked
     for a connection when the machine had no port left (`EADDRNOTAVAIL`), and the exit with that error waited,
     as it must, for the server, which waited for the clients that would never come.
1684. **The server closes first in the echoes**: it sends back what a client sends, `SIZE` times `ROUNDS` bytes,
     and lets the connection go; the client reads once more and sees the end. The side that closes first waits
     a while (TIME_WAIT) before its port is free again, and macOS has 16,384 ports for connections: with the
     clients closing first, 1,000 connections run six times by each of five builds took them all within the
     wait. The same in the three languages, the async way and the blocking.
1685. **A run that fails stops the measuring**: a measured run that exits with a code other than 0 stops it, so
     that a program that gave up early is never counted fast; until now only the checks looked at the code.
1686. **An exit with an error will cancel the tasks it lets go, then wait for them**, in a slice of its own, as
     Swift's task groups, Kotlin's `coroutineScope` and Trio's nurseries have it: a wait of the network, the
     clock or a channel in them ends at once with `Cancelled`, and each goes out by its own way of an error, so
     no task is cut off without being told and none holds the program for ever. When such an error comes
     depends on the system (16,384 ports on macOS, about 28,000 on Linux), and a program that hangs on one
     machine and not on another is a fault of the language in the eyes of the one who runs it. A task of
     `Task.run` held in a call that blocks cannot be cut, as in Swift and Kotlin; the first step is the async
     tasks.
1687. **A socket found empty or full waits for the loop at once**, on Linux and macOS, as Tokio does on epoll and
     kqueue: a send the socket takes only part of waits rather than try again and be told so, and a receive
     that gives fewer bytes than there was room for marks the socket not ready, unless the loop has seen it
     ready since, so the next receive waits without a call of the system. Windows, whose driver answers each
     question once, is left as it is.
1688. **A hex number and an address are written where they go**: `{n:x}`, `{n:X}` and `{n:x4}` in `$"..."` are
     written by the code that writes the line, as decimal numbers already were, not through a format read at
     run time; `SocketAddress` writes its host straight into the `Formatter`, where it made a text for the host
     and one for each v4 part (`host()` goes through the same writing). Ten million addresses took 2.262 s and
     take 1.487, Zig 1.535 and Rust 2.006.
1689. **Written as they are**: SG's lines read from a connection are text, each checked to be UTF-8 as Rust's
     `read_line` checks it, and take 1.34 to 1.39 times Zig's bytes, the time of Zig without the check; the
     byte reader of the work on servers will give bytes. The async stream of 64 KB pieces takes 1.12 times
     Tokio's time and 50,000 connections async 1.10 times Zig's, the time being in the calls of the system and
     most likely in where the tasks run; written under "Slower than C" for the work on servers, measured on
     Linux, since on the network SG is never to be worse than the best rival.
1690. **The frame of an async task carries less for the network**: every call of the network in an async
     function waits in one room of its frame, since one waits at a time and what it gives is taken out at
     once; that room is 224 bytes where it was 600, the host and the port of a `connect` (270 bytes) made for
     the call alone and the address of a datagram 28 bytes, a v4 or a v6 one, where the system's
     `sockaddr_storage` takes 128. The tasks of an echo went from 2,248 and 1,536 bytes to 672 and 560, and
     1,000 connections at once from 6.6 MB to 3.8 MB, Tokio's; the time is the same.

The trial is tested by `the_trial_of_net` (new): the SG program, interpreted and compiled, async and blocking,
echoes, connects, streams, reads lines with `\r\n` and an empty one, sends datagrams, and reads and writes eight
addresses (three not addresses) as Rust, Zig and Python's `ipaddress` do. The same program compiled for Windows
on Arm gives the same output. The whole of the tests passes on macOS and on Linux, with 508 of the 1926
programs compiled through C and run clean under the sanitizers, 109 of them under the thread sanitizer.

## 155. An exit with an error lets its tasks go

Choices made in the hundred-and-fifty-fifth slice (an exit with an error lets its tasks go), which builds 1686,
approved on 22 November 2026; 1696 mends a fault of 1687 the tests of this slice found on Linux:

1691. **What is let go**: an exit with an error lets go the tasks it did not await, those in variables and
     those left in lists of tasks, and the tasks they wait for in turn: a task let go that awaits a task, or
     waits in `Task.whenAny` or `Task.whenAll`, lets those go too. `Task.whenAll` still waits for every task
     and gives what each gave, errors among them; a program that stops at the first error empties the list
     with `Task.whenAny` and `try`, and the tasks left in it are let go. No task is let go because another
     failed.
1692. **What letting go does**: every wait in the task that can say it was cancelled ends at once with
     `Cancelled`: the network, files and pipes, other programs, a name looked up, and a delay or a channel
     given a token. The mark stays: every such wait the task starts afterwards ends at once too. The task goes
     out by its own way of an error; nothing is cut in the middle of a line.
1693. **What is not let go**: a delay or a channel wait without a token, which cannot say it was cancelled,
     and which, ended early, would turn a loop that waits into one that spins; work that never waits; a task
     of `Task.run` held in a call that blocks, as in Swift and Kotlin. They run on and are waited for; a
     program that needs them to end gives them a token.
1694. **What it costs**: a mark of 4 bytes in each task. A wait looks at it when it starts and when it is
     woken and not over; a wait that is not cut takes no lock more, and the cut, done by the task itself on its
     own wait, takes the clock's lock as a cancel of a token does. The interpreter does the same: the fibers of
     the tasks let go, and the tasks each fiber waits for, are kept by the scheduler.
1695. **What is tested**: the night of slice 154, a server that waits for clients while the function fails
     before any comes, ends with the error at once, where it waited for ever; a task let go lets go the task
     it awaits; a wait started after the mark ends at once; a delay given a token ends and one without runs
     on; `Task.whenAny` with `try` lets the other tasks go; a channel given a token ends; a function that is not
     async lets its task go, interpreted and compiled alike (`ok_async_error_cuts`). The task of
     `ok_async_writer_drop` that reads a file after its delay, let go, now finds the read cancelled, and the
     async methods of a writer in compiled code look at the mark as the interpreter's do.
1696. **A connection that the other side closed is never marked empty**: the loop keeps that the other side
     closed, or that the socket failed (EPOLLRDHUP, EPOLLHUP, EPOLLERR; kqueue's EV_EOF), in the slot of the
     socket, and it stays, as Tokio keeps its READ_CLOSED. Since 1687 a receive that gave fewer bytes than there
     was room for marked the socket not ready; when the other side's close had come before those last bytes were
     read, its end was still there to read, no edge would tell of it again, and the task waited for ever: on
     Linux the compiled stream of `the_trial_of_net` hung in about one run of ten. The programs of the trials in
     `tests/cli.rs` are now killed after five minutes, and the test says which hung, where one waited 46 minutes
     unseen.

The whole of the tests passes on macOS and on Linux, with 509 of the 1927 programs compiled through C and run
clean under the sanitizers, 110 of them under the thread sanitizer; the new test runs the same on Windows on Arm.

## The golden tests, several at a time

Approved on 22 November 2026, after the time of the tests was measured:

1697. **The golden tests build and run their programs several at a time**: the tests of `tests/golden.rs`
     (`compiled`, `compiled_small_pools`, `compiled_tests`, `golden`, `modules` and the check of 128 bits)
     share one count of places, as many as the machine has threads, and each takes the next program when a
     place is free; what each program gave is put back in the order of the files, so a report reads as before.
     Measured on macOS (16 threads) before the change: the file took 773 s, all of it one program after
     another; `compiled` alone 773 s, of which the C compiler 311 s (164 s under the address sanitizer) and the
     programs 423 s, most of it waiting (a program under the thread sanitizer ran 1.4 s on average). After it
     the file takes 109 s, with every test passing; twice as many places gave 101 s and slowed the
     interpreter's tests, so the count stays at the threads. What is left is mostly the C compiler: building
     the C runtime once for each configuration, in place of in every program, waits as the next cut. The
     longest run in `modules` is the test command of `examples/zlib` in the interpreter (43 s: the language's
     zlib at every level on 360 KB), shorter than `compiled` and so not yet in the way.

The whole of the tests passes on macOS and on Linux, run side by side as before a commit: `cargo test` took
2 min 49 s on macOS (about 23 min before) and 2 min 36 s on Linux (30 to 40 min before), with the same 509
programs compiled, run under the sanitizers and looked at for leaks.

## 156. The server lane: servers measured against Rust, Zig and C

Approved on 22 November 2026, before any number was seen (the long run of 1707 and the HTTP server of 1706
were asked for while it was being built):

1698. **A slice that measures**: neither the language nor the library changes for the numbers; a mistake met on
     the way is mended (below), and every case where SG is worse than the best rival goes into
     [Slower than C](../../benchmarks/slower-than-c.md) as a mistake of the server lane, to be mended in the
     slices that follow.
1699. **Linux is the reference.** The servers are measured on Linux, in a virtual machine on the Mac (podman,
     8 cores): the server on cores 0-3 and the load on cores 4-7 (`taskset`), every rival in the same machine.
     macOS is not the reference for servers.
1700. **One load for every server**, written in C (`trials/server/load/load.c`, epoll and kqueue): a closed loop
     for the answers a second, and an open loop at a fixed rate whose time of each answer is counted from when
     its request was due, not from when it went, so that a server that stalls is charged for every request
     that waited behind the stall (coordinated omission); a histogram gives p50, p99, p99.9 and the most. It
     checks every answer byte for byte where asked, opens idle connections from several addresses of the
     loopback, and was held once against `redis-benchmark` and `wrk` (they agree within 3% where the server is
     the limit; with the fastest servers they reach their own limit first).
1701. **A store that speaks a part of RESP2**, Redis's protocol (PING, GET, SET, DEL), the same in SG, Rust
     (`tokio`), Zig (`std.Io`) and C (epoll, a thread on each core with a listener of its own, `SO_REUSEPORT`):
     64 maps each behind a lock, the map chosen by the FNV-1a of the key; each connection reads into a buffer of
     4 KB that grows when one command does not fit, answers every whole command it holds and sends the answers
     together; a command of more than 1,024 parts or a part of more than 1 MB closes the connection. Zig 0.17.1's
     `Io.Evented` (io_uring) has no `netAccept` yet, so Zig's servers run on `Io.Threaded`, a thread for each
     connection.
1702. **The cases**, chosen before the numbers: 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000 idle connections (the memory each adds,
     and the processor's time of 10 seconds with all of them silent); 1, 50 and 1,000 connections, with 1 and 16
     requests in flight on each; 100 connections at 10%, 40% and 70% of the slowest server's answers a second;
     10,000 connections that each ask once a second; 10,000 idle connections beside 100 at 70%; values of 4 KB
     and 64 KB.
1703. **Published in [Servers against Rust, Zig and C](../../benchmarks/server.md)**, every case, those SG
     loses too, from one command (`python3 trials/server/measure.py`), shown to the user before anything was
     committed.
1704. **The servers are a test**: `tests/cli.rs` builds the SG servers and the load, and checks every answer
     on every run of the tests (Linux and macOS; the load waits on epoll or kqueue, so not on Windows).
1705. **The slices after it follow the numbers**: the scheduler first (below), then the library a server needs
     (time limits, a limit on connections, a graceful end, a reader that lends slices, `writev`, a group of
     tasks that takes its ended tasks out by itself), then the tests of robustness with a job of Linux in the CI,
     then HTTP/1.1, `sendfile`, io_uring and TLS through C.
1706. **An HTTP server that answers GET /ping**, connections kept open, as a second server: in SG and C written
     by hand and reading every header, in Rust hyper 1, in Zig `std.http.Server`; with 50 and 1,000
     connections, a connection for each request, and at fixed rates.
1707. **A long run**: half the slowest's answers a second for 30 minutes on 1,000 connections, each opened again
     after 1,000 answers; the server looked at every 10 seconds (memory, threads, open files, processor), the
     time of the answers in each 10 seconds, and 30 seconds after the load stops, so that memory that grows,
     files that are not let go or a server that slows as it runs are seen.

What the numbers say (one run of each case, and the long run for SG and Rust; the tables are in [Servers
against Rust, Zig and C](../../benchmarks/server.md)): SG holds the least memory of the three languages in
every case but two, where `tokio` holds less (10,000 connections asking once a second, 50.5 MB to 53.2; the
long run, 13.3 MB to 22.2), holds 100,000 idle connections at 4.96 KB each
(Rust 5.00, C 4.17, of which the buffer of 4 KB; Zig's thread for each connection stops before 100,000), and
gives the best tail where many connections wait: 10,000 connections asking once a second answer within 2.3 ms
at p99, where `tokio` takes 3.9 s and Zig 1.7 s. With few connections it is slow: 50 connections get 0.64 times
the answers of Zig's threads and 0.39 times C's. On one core SG answers 396,000 a second, more than C (349,000)
and Rust (360,000); on four C answers nearly four times as many and SG and `tokio` not even one and a half.
The difference is in the kernel, with the same calls of the system for each answer: SG's loop of the network is
waited on by one thread, which wakes the tasks of the sockets onto its ring, from which the other threads take
them, so each answer is read on one core and served on another (a wake of `futex`, the socket's data in
another's cache). C keeps each connection on the thread whose loop took it. That is the first mistake to mend.
In the long run of 30 minutes neither SG nor `tokio` grows: memory, files open and the tail of the fifth minute
are those of the last, and SG's p99 stays at 1.1 ms where `tokio`'s is near a second.

Mended on the way:

- `if r is Ok(text)` on a `Result<read string, E>` named a `read read string`, which went nowhere a `read string`
  goes; `match` named it right. A name of a pattern under `read` is now the borrow the value is, when it is one
  (`tests/cases/ok_is_borrowed_payload.sg`).
- The pool of threads counted the cores of the machine, not the cores the program may run on: under `taskset`,
  or in a container given two cores of sixty-four, it started a thread for every core of the machine. On Linux it
  now counts the cores of its affinity and no more than the quota of its cgroup (`cpu.max`), as Rust's
  `available_parallelism` does.
- `ok_net_errors` connected to a port it had just let go to see a refusal; since the golden tests run several at a
  time, another test of the network took that port at once and answered. It now connects to port 1.

Found, for the slices that follow (in [Slower than C](../../benchmarks/slower-than-c.md)): the scheduler above;
`Task.whenAny` looks for the ended task through the list and moves the rest (a server of many connections pays
for each one that ends), and a server takes each connection through one task; no `TCP_NODELAY` and no way to
ask for it; `string` has no `hash()` to call; a map keyed by bytes and looked up by a slice is missing, so the
store's keys are text (a key that is not UTF-8 is answered with an error); with 100,000 idle connections SG
spends 0.45 s of the processor in 10 s, `tokio` 0.36 and C 0.22. One connection gets 27,000 answers a second from SG and
34,000 from Zig, and HTTP at 70% has a p99 of 292 µs in SG and 189 µs in hyper. The median of three runs and
the long runs of Zig, C and HTTP are still to be taken.

## 157. The scheduler of the server lane: a loop of the network on each thread

Approved on 22 November 2026, after the numbers of slice 156:

1708. **A loop on each thread.** Each thread of the pool has a loop of the network of its own (epoll, kqueue,
     an I/O completion port), and a thread with nothing to do waits on its own loop, not on a condition.
1709. **Woken through its loop.** A thread that work comes to from outside is woken through its loop: an
     `eventfd` on Linux, `EVFILT_USER` on macOS, a packet on Windows. The hand-over of the one loop between
     threads, and the wake of a condition that came with it, are gone.
1710. **A socket stays where it is first waited for.** The first time a task waits for a socket, the socket
     goes on the loop of the thread that holds the fewest (this thread's when it holds no more than the
     others), and stays there; when its task is woken it runs on that thread. Even behind one task that
     accepts, the connections are spread over the cores, as C's listener on each thread (`SO_REUSEPORT`)
     spreads them.
1711. **A busy thread's loop is looked at.** When a thread is busy with one long task and its loop has not
     been looked at for a while, the loop is looked at without waiting and what is ready is taken (Linux
     lets a thread wait on another's epoll), so a long task does not keep the other connections of its
     thread waiting: the promise that held with one loop holds.
1712. **The delays stay shared.** The clock keeps one heap of delays; one thread waits for the nearest, the
     others without a time.
1713. **Windows the same way**, an I/O completion port on each thread, as mio does; the three systems in this
     slice.
1714. **The aim**: with 50 connections at least Zig's answers a second (about 900,000), no case worse than
     before, and p99 no worse than the rivals'; short cases measured by day, the whole by night.

As built:

- The loops are opened, and every thread of the pool started, when a program first waits for a socket or for
  the end of a program; the ends of programs wait on the loop of the first thread, and the waits a cancel
  ended are let go of after a round of that loop. One thread at a time is in a round of a loop.
- 1711 is kept by the clock's thread, not by a thread of the pool: a thread of the pool that watched while it
  waited would have to hand the watch over each time work came to it, a wake each time. The clock's thread
  looks at the loops every millisecond while a thread of the pool is awake, every 2, 4, up to 10 ms while
  none is, and stops after a second with none, as Go's `sysmon` slows down; a thread that wakes then starts it
  again. A loop of a thread that is awake and has had no round since the last look is looked at without
  waiting, and the tasks it wakes go on the shared queue, for a thread that is free. The clock's thread was
  already the one that waits for the nearest delay (1712). `compiled_long_task_leaves_its_loop` holds a
  thread with one task until a datagram on that thread's loop is heard; without the watch it never ends.
- A thread whose queue is empty looks at its own loop without waiting before it takes another thread's
  tasks, which run better where they are; a look that finds nothing leaves the next four out. Without the
  look, 50 connections got 1,219,000 answers a second, and 2,123,000 with 16 requests in flight on each; with
  a look every time, 1,432,000 and 1,977,000; with four left out after a look that found nothing, 1,414,000
  and 2,035,000 (medians of three runs).
- Another thread is woken to take tasks a loop woke when more than one waits on the ring: with eight, 50
  connections got 1,301,000 answers a second and 1,000 connections 1,209,000, against 1,414,000 and 1,332,000.

What the numbers say ([Servers](../../benchmarks/server.md), one run of each case): with 50 connections SG's
store answers 1,342,000 times a second, where it answered 578,000 (Zig 903,000, `tokio` 600,000, C 1,478,000),
HTTP 1,386,000 where it answered 581,000, values of 4 KB 1,050,000, more than C; the aim of 1714 on answers a
second is met. Two parts of the aim are not, and are kept in [Slower than C](../../benchmarks/slower-than-c.md)
to be mended later:

- At 70% of the slowest's answers a second the p99 grew: HTTP 441 µs to 1.01 ms (hyper 195 µs), 10,000 idle
  connections beside 100 busy ones 364 to 805 µs (`tokio` 339 µs). C, with a loop on each thread, is near SG
  (997 and 668 µs); the p50 is lower than `tokio`'s. Neither a look at the other threads' loops before
  sleeping, nor 50 µs more of looking at its own, nor the watch taken out mended it.
- Where both ends talk inside one program, they lie on the loops of two threads and each message wakes another
  core: in `trials/std/net` the async echo of 64 bytes takes 1.546 s, where it took 1.208 s (`tokio` 1.185 s),
  and on Linux `udpecho` 0.78 s where it took 0.41 s. On one loop they would be fast again (0.45 s), but a
  server would not be spread; a socket on the loop of the thread that first waits for it spreads neither
  (382,000 answers a second with 50 connections).

The stream of 64 KB pieces got faster (0.140 s against 0.172 s, `tokio` 0.153 s), and so did 50,000
connections one after another (4.000 s against 4.460 s, Zig 3.972 s). On macOS `asynccopy`, which has no
socket, takes 0.59 times C where it took 1.04 (0.170 s against 0.283 s), not looked into yet.
