## 145. A const calls no function

Choices made in the hundred-and-forty-fifth slice (a const calls no function). 1606 was approved during the
hundred-and-forty-fourth slice, 1607 and 1608 at the start of this one:

1606. **A const calls no function.** Its value is made of numbers, texts, other consts, operators and casts,
     and arrays, structs and enums of them, as in C#, Go and C. A call is refused at its name (`a const calls
     no function`, with the hint of a `static`); the default value of a field, the number of a variant and a
     `const assert` say it in their own words, and `int[Count]` from a const that calls says it too. This
     takes back the calls of the hundred-and-fourth slice (1100 to 1108, 1124 to 1134): the rule of what may
     run and its errors, which showed the calls that lead there; the ban of `sin` and its kin; the budget of
     100 000 000 steps for a const; the 64 MB at which a growing text or list stopped (1338); a const that
     reaches itself through the functions it calls; and the bodies that waited for a const and were checked
     again. With them go 1159 (one rule for a `const assert` that measures a type is now every one's), 1125
     and 1136, which were the limits of those calls. Every const is found once, before any body, from what is
     written; of `comptime.rs` only the search for the statics a function reads is left (`static_reads.rs`).
     A call ran the program inside the compiler: `sg lsp`, the check of a package or a build could be held
     for seconds and gigabytes by a program it only reads, and the language had a second way, beside
     `static`, to find a value with a function.
1607. **The check of a pattern stays.** A pattern written out in the program (`Regex.of("...")`) is checked
     when the program is compiled by the `std` module's own `Regex.problem`, which the interpreter runs: the
     one place the compiler runs code, and never the program's. The budget of 100 000 000 steps (1 000 000
     when the front end is fuzzed) is kept for it alone; past it the error is `checking this takes more than
     100 000 000 steps`.
1608. **The tables of `std` are written out.** The table of CRC-32 (4096 entries, `Crc32Tables` in
     `checksum.sg`) and the four of deflate (`compression.sg`) were consts found by calls. They are written
     between marked lines by `tools/std_tables.py`, as zlib writes `crc32.h`, with the values the functions
     found, and `tools/std_tables.py --check` says whether the files hold what it makes. A `static` would
     have cost every call of `crc32` a load and a branch.

`const_steps_run_out` became `const_that_would_spin_is_refused` (a const that would never end is refused at
its call, at once). `error_const_method_call` and `error_field_default_call` are new; `error_const_call` (was
`error_comptime_sin`), `error_const_count_call` (was `error_comptime_type_count`), `error_const_too_big` (was
`error_comptime_size`, its array written out), `error_const_assert_call` (was
`error_const_assert_call_measures`), `error_enum_number_calls` (the call in the number itself) and
`error_const_counted` changed. `ok_comptime`, 14 `error_comptime_*`, `error_static_in_comptime`,
`error_const_call_sizeof`, `error_const_too_big_repeat` and `error_const_too_big_text` are removed: each
now says only that a const calls no function, and what they tried through a call (a circle, an overflow, an
index outside) `error_const_cycle`, `error_const_overflow` and `error_const_index` try without one.
`ok_const_assert`, `ok_field_defaults` and the module `consts` write out what they called. The generator of
programs (`fuzz/difference`) writes a const of operators whose operands are masked so that none overflows,
where it wrote `wrappingAdd` and its kin: 1730 programs, and 421 that borrow, none refused and no
difference found. The whole of the tests passes on macOS and on Linux (the kernel's
test, which needs a Clang that knows RISC-V, on Linux alone), with 492 of the 1887 programs compiled through C
and run clean under the sanitizers, 108 of them under the thread sanitizer.

## 146. A `ref` range counted in a call, `copyFrom`, the Pike machine

Choices made in the hundred-and-forty-sixth slice (a range lent as `ref` is counted among what a call
lends; `copyFrom`; a Pike machine that copies a thread's slots once). 1597 and 1598 were approved during the
hundred-and-forty-second slice, 1609 and 1610 at the start of this one:

1597. **`dst.copyFrom(src)` copies the items of a window over as many.** `dst` is a list, an array, a
     `ref T[]` or a range in front of the method, lent as `ref` as in front of `sort`
     (`buf[0..n].copyFrom(block[8..8 + n])`); `src` is a `read T[]`; the items are `Copyable`, and a type
     whose items carry borrows waits, as for `addAll`. Lengths that differ are a panic: `` `copyFrom` is
     given 2 item(s) to copy over 3 ``, with the note of a window of as many, as Rust's `copy_from_slice`
     and Zig's `@memcpy` stop; Go's `copy` copies the shorter length, which hides the mistake. Through a
     `read T[]` it is the error of `sort`; `copyFromSlice` and `copy_from_slice` are errors whose hint is
     `copyFrom`. Compiled code tests the lengths and calls `memmove` once. The loops of `std` that copied
     item by item are calls of it: the window and the input of deflate, the input and output of inflate
     and of `ZipReader`, a stored block, the slots of the Pike machine, the block of SHA-256 and HMAC, and
     the groups of an IPv6 address. The copy of a match of deflate (LZ77), whose source may be written by
     the copy itself, stays a loop. A pattern that keeps many threads alive with many groups (`(a?)` written
     4,000 times, then `a` 4,000 times, on 4,000 `a`s) takes 60.6 s where it took 138.6 s, compiled with
     `--release` on an Apple M3 Max; the memory is the same, 1.04 GB. The copies of a thread's
     slots that are left go with 1598.
1609. **The two windows of `copyFrom` may be of one place.** `buf[0..n].copyFrom(buf[k..k + n])` moves
     items inside one list or array, forward or back: the items given are read before any is written over,
     as C's `memmove` reads them. It is the one exception to "a `ref` borrow is the only one", and a safe one:
     a `Copyable` item holds nothing a write could free, and nothing grows. Rust has a second method for it
     (`copy_within`), Zig a second builtin (`@memmove`); Go's `copy` allows it, as here.

1598. **Pike's machine copies a thread's slots once, as Rust's PikeVM does.** The closure of a thread, which
     follows its splits, jumps and saves, now walks on the thread's own slots: a save writes over one and the
     machine's stack gives the old value back as it unwinds, and the slots are copied only to a step that
     reads a character or matches. Before, they were copied out to a scratch row and back for every thread.
     `captures` and `replaceAll` find where the match starts and ends first, as Rust's do: by the DFAs, or,
     when the DFAs give up (a pattern of thousands of steps empties them too often), by Pike's machine with
     two slots, which copies little; the machine of every group's slots then starts its threads at that start
     alone and stops at that end. Before, DFAs that gave up left that machine to search from the text's
     start, starting a thread at each place: half as many copies again. `replaceAll` ran Pike's machine
     alone for each match; it now goes through the DFAs as `find` does, and keeps its machine in the
     `Regex` as `captures` does. The slots of the threads of the machine's two lists are kept in blocks of
     16 threads, one list's after the other's: slots copied to the place right after them copied 1.5 times
     slower, measured in C on the same machine. `(a?)` written 4,000 times, then `a` 4,000 times, on 4,000
     `a`s, compiled with `--release` on an Apple M3 Max: 13.9 s and 0.53 GB, from 60.6 s and 1.04 GB.
     Rust's `regex` 1.13.2 takes 27.0 s and 2.57 GB as it comes, and 13.8 s and 2.62 GB when its DFAs may
     take a gigabyte (`dfa_size_limit`); the two copy the same bytes, 16 million copies of 64 KB. Rust keeps
     slots for every state of the program, SG for the threads alive. At 1,000 groups the two take 0.37 s and
     0.33 s.

1610. **A range lent as `ref` lends its whole list or array.** `ref xs[0..2]` is kept as the range alone, and
     the check of what one call lends did not see it: beside it, another range of the same list
     (`f(ref xs[0..2], xs[1..3])`), the list itself (`read xs`) or an item of it (`read xs[2]`) was taken.
     The function could write over an item the other argument still read: in compiled code, a text freed
     under a `read string` that looked at it. A range lent as `ref` is now one of the places a call lends,
     counted as its list or array, as an item is, so two ranges of one place clash in one call however far
     apart they are, as in Rust. Two `ref` windows of one place at once (Rust's `split_at_mut`) are not in
     the language yet.

Tested by `error_ref_range_beside_range` and `error_ref_range_beside_whole` (new), both taken on the code
before this slice, and `error_ref_range_twice` (new): two `ref` ranges were refused before, by the rule of the
parts of a call, and are now lent twice. The generator of programs that borrow (`fuzz/difference`) lends a
range of a list as `ref` beside a range that may be of the same list: on the code before the fix, 89 of 648
programs read freed memory under the address sanitizer; after it, none of 1061. `ok_copy_from`,
`panic_copy_from_lengths`, `error_copy_from_text`, `error_copy_from_read` and `error_copy_from_while_lent`
(new) try `copyFrom`. The whole of the tests passes on macOS and on Linux, with 494 of the 1895 programs
compiled through C and run clean under the sanitizers, 108 of them under the thread sanitizer. One run on Linux
saw `ok_net_errors` connect to the port of a listener it had closed, which another test had taken in the
meantime; the test passed 60 times of 60 alone and the whole run passed again.

1598 is tested by RE2's search tests, interpreted and compiled, and by `a_regex_dfa_that_gives_up` in
`tests/cli.rs`, which now takes groups and a replacement from a match that starts after the text's start,
where the DFAs give up; what it prints is what Rust's `regex` prints. 30,072 random patterns and texts, among
them patterns of up to 4,000 groups that the DFAs give up on, give the same groups and replacements as the
code before this change, compiled; 3,000 of them interpreted too. They take 2.1 s where they took 5.8 s.
The whole of the tests passes again on macOS and on Linux, with the same 494 of 1895 programs compiled
through C and run clean under the sanitizers, 108 of them under the thread sanitizer.

## 147. The normal forms of Unicode

Choices made in the hundred-and-forty-seventh slice (the normal forms of Unicode). 1599 was approved during
the hundred-and-forty-second slice, 1611 to 1615 at the start of this one:

1599. **`text.normalize(form)` gives the text in a normal form of Unicode; `text.isNormalized(form)` says
     whether it is in one.** The forms are those of UAX #15, a `NormalizationForm` of `std` with C#'s names:
     `C` (NFC), `D` (NFD), `KC` (NFKC) and `KD` (NFKD), always written, since no form is right for every use.
     `"e\u{301}".normalize(NormalizationForm.C)` is `"é"`, and `"ﬁ²".normalize(NormalizationForm.KC)` is
     `"fi2"`. Rust has them in the `unicode-normalization` crate (`s.nfc()`, `is_nfc(s)`), Go in
     `golang.org/x/text/unicode/norm`, C# as `Normalize` and `IsNormalized`. `nfc()`, `toNFC()`, `is_nfc()`
     and their like are errors whose hint is the call. A form given as a text (`"NFC"`) is a type error.
1611. **The work is the compiler's own, as the case of a text is.** The interpreter in Rust and compiled
     code in C read the same tables of `src/unicode_tables.rs`, which `tools/unicode_tables.py` now also
     makes from `DerivedNormalizationProps.txt` of the UCD 17.0, held to its SHA-256 as the four other files
     are: a record for each code point, found in three steps as the record of case is (its canonical
     combining class, the bits of its quick checks, and where its full canonical and compatibility
     decompositions are, each already in the canonical order), 6,763 code points of decompositions and the
     961 pairs that compose. The syllables of Hangul are decomposed and composed by Hangul's rule. In
     compiled code the tables (96 KB) are only in a program that normalizes, apart from the tables of case.
     `NormalizationForm` is in `std/unicode.sg`, beside `UnicodeCategory`; its `normalize` and
     `isNormalized` call `System.normalize` and `System.isNormalized`, as `c.category()` calls
     `System.charCategory`.
1612. **The quick check first.** A text is looked at character by character (ASCII a byte at a time); a
     text whose characters the quick check of the form says Yes to, with the marks in order, is copied as
     it is. Otherwise the text up to the last starter before that place is copied, and the rest is
     normalized a piece at a time, each piece ending before a starter the check says Yes to, which nothing
     before it reorders or composes with. `isNormalized` is the quick check; when it says Maybe (a character
     that may compose with the one before it), the text normalized is compared with the text. ICU works so,
     and the quick checks are Unicode's own (`NFC_QC` and the others). After each piece the quick check
     looks again from the starter that ended it, and what it says Yes to is copied as it is: a text with a
     few characters to change is mostly copied. 16 MB of text in eight languages (French, Vietnamese,
     German, Greek, Korean, English, Portuguese and Spanish, Turkish), compiled with `--release` on an
     Apple M3 Max, the best of ten runs, each output the same bytes as Python's `unicodedata`:

     | Case | SG | Rust `unicode-normalization` 0.1.26 | Rust `icu_normalizer` 2.4.0 | ICU4C 79.1 |
     |---|---|---|---|---|
     | NFC of the text in NFD | 55 ms | 147 ms | 66 ms | 117 ms |
     | NFD of the text in NFC | 36 ms | 106 ms | 29 ms | 81 ms |
     | NFC of the text in NFC | 7 ms | 149 ms | 8 ms | 62 ms |
     | NFKC of the text in NFC | 7 ms | 147 ms | 9 ms | 62 ms |

     `unicode-normalization` is the crate most Rust programs use, called as its users call it
     (`s.nfc().collect::<String>()`), with no quick check of its own; `icu_normalizer` is ICU4X, Unicode's
     own library for Rust. ICU4C normalizes UTF-16, and its time counts the text made UTF-16 and back. Before
     the quick check looked again after each piece, SG took 121, 80, 7 and 118 ms: one `᾽` (U+1FBD, which
     NFKC changes) in the first lines sent the whole rest down the slow way. NFD is the case SG loses
     (1.24 times `icu_normalizer`): written in "Slower than C".
1613. **The output is always Unicode's, and a run of marks never takes quadratic time.** Go's `norm` puts
     U+034F COMBINING GRAPHEME JOINER after 30 marks in a row (the Stream-Safe Text Format of UAX #15),
     which bounds its buffer and changes the text; SG does not. The marks of a run are put in the order of
     their classes by a stable sort: by insertion when the run is short, by merging when it is long. `a`
     followed by 100,000 marks of two classes is normalized at once, interpreted and compiled.
1614. **Unicode's own tests.** `tests/normalization-suite` holds `NormalizationTest.txt` of Unicode 17.0,
     unchanged, under the Unicode licence. `check.sg` puts each of the five texts of each of its 20,034 lines
     in the four forms and holds the results to the invariants of its header, holds `isNormalized` to
     `normalize`, and checks that the 1,094,978 code points Part 1 does not name are left as they are; it
     runs interpreted and compiled (`a_normalization_suite`), and the same runs through the interpreter's
     side alone (`normalization_suite` in `src/unicode.rs`). `tools/unicode_tables.py` checks every code
     point against Python 3.15's `unicodedata`, which knows Unicode 17.0: the combining class, NFD and NFKD
     of the character alone, each pair that composes, and that there is no other.
1615. **Zip names are compared by Unicode's canonical caseless match.** The key that tells two names apart
     is the name in NFD, folded as `(?i)` folds it, and in NFD again (Unicode's definition D145): `é` as one
     character and as `e` and an accent is one name, and the second is `DuplicateName`, in `ZipWriter` and in
     `ZipReader`, as macOS takes the two for one file. The note of 1594 that left them as two names is gone
     from the library page.

Tested by `ok_unicode_normalize`, `error_unicode_normalize_no_form`, `error_unicode_normalize_form_type` and
`error_unicode_normalize_other_name` (new), and by `ok_zip`, which adds `çay/şeker.txt` written decomposed
and in upper case: both are `DuplicateName`, and the archive is the same bytes. The suite, the program of
100,000 marks and the examples run clean under the address and undefined-behaviour sanitizers. The whole of
the tests passes on macOS and on Linux, with 495 of the 1899 programs compiled through C and run clean under
the sanitizers, 108 of them under the thread sanitizer.

## 148. The default values of parameters, the depth of written JSON

Choices made in the hundred-and-forty-eighth slice (the default values of parameters, and the depth of
JSON that is written). 1600 was approved during the hundred-and-forty-second slice; 1616 to 1619 at the start
of this one, and 1620 to 1623 after them, when the user chose to give the methods there were a parameter of
options instead of new methods; 1616 fell with that. 1624 to 1628 came while it was built, approved the same day:

1620. **The last parameters of a function may have default values**, written as C# writes them:
     `void connect(read string host, int port = 80, int retries = 3)`. A call leaves arguments out only at
     its end, so a parameter after one with a default value has one too (`` `port` comes after `host`, which
     has a default value, so it needs one too ``). There are no named arguments. The language had no
     overloads and no default values (04, "No overloads"); this was the second of the two doors written
     there, and costs nothing at run time. Rust, Go, Zig and C have neither; Rust and Go write a second name
     (`to_string_pretty`, `MarshalIndent`) or a struct of options, Zig a struct of options given on every
     call (`.{}`), C#, C++ and Kotlin default values.
1621. **A default value is what the default value of a field can be**: a value found when the program is
     compiled, held by a const of its own, or `null`, or an empty collection. The call that leaves the
     argument out gets it as if it were written there: the compiled C of `v.toJson()` and of
     `v.toJson(JsonOptions {})` is the same but for the text of the place a panic names. A call is refused as
     in a const (`the default value of a parameter calls no function`, 1606): a value found when the program
     runs is given by the call. The names in a default are found where it is written, so a function of
     another module may have a private const as its default (`ok_param_defaults_module`).
1622. **Where.** A function, a method, a function of a type and `create` have them; a parameter taken by value
     or with `read`. A `ref` parameter has none (it is given a place, not a value), nor a function of C (C
     is given every argument), nor a method the language calls by itself, which is given every argument:
     `format`, `equals`, `hash`, `compare` and `dispose`. A call through a `Func` gives every argument: the
     value does not know the defaults of the function it holds, as a pointer to a function of C does not.
1623. **The token of `std` is a default value, and `toJsonIndented` is gone.** A function of `std` whose last
     parameter was `read CancellationToken` could be called without it, by a rule of the checker for `std`
     alone (627); the eleven of them now write `read CancellationToken token = CancellationToken.None`, the
     rule is gone, and a function of the program can do the same. The built-in async functions written in
     Rust (404) take their token or not, as before, as if declared so. JSON has one method to write and one
     to read: `v.toJson(JsonOptions options = JsonOptions {})` and `Json.parse(read text, JsonOptions options
     = JsonOptions {})`; `v.toJsonIndented(2)` is `v.toJson(JsonOptions { indent = 2 })`.
1600. **The JSON that is written stops at the depth that is read**: 128 levels of arrays and objects, and one
     more is a panic, as writing NaN is: `JSON nested deeper than 128 cannot be written: toJson(JsonOptions {
     maxDepth = ... }) writes deeper`. What `toJson` writes, `Json.parse` reads back. serde_json writes any
     depth and reads 128 unless `disable_recursion_limit` is called; C#'s `System.Text.Json` stops at 64 both
     ways unless `MaxDepth` says another depth.
1617. **`JsonOptions`** has `int maxDepth = 128` and `int? indent = null`: `null` writes on one line, a number
     of spaces on lines, as `toJsonIndented` did. A `maxDepth` below 1 or an indentation below 0 is a panic,
     in writing and in reading; there is no 0 that means "the default", as C# has.
1618. **`JsonErrorKind.TooDeep(int limit)`**: the error says the depth it stopped at (`nested deeper than 4`).
1619. **Reading and writing count alike**: with `maxDepth = n`, n arrays and objects in one another are
     written and read, the next is a panic in writing and a `TooDeep` in reading. There is no upper bound:
     each level takes some of the stack, in reading and in writing, which the page says.
1624. **`T {}`, a literal that gives no field, and `CancellationToken.None` are default values too**, put
     into the call as written, as `null` is: a const cannot hold an `int?` (`JsonOptions` has one) or a
     token. `T {}` is made of the default values of its fields, each found already, and it is checked where
     it is written (`` `Range` is missing `low` ``). A field may have one as its default as well.
1625. **`JsonOptions` is `Copyable` and given by value**, not with `read`: a value made in the call is lent
     only with `read` written (`read JsonOptions { indent = 2 }`), and its 12 bytes cost no more than a pointer.
1626. **A struct of `std` whose fields are all public, and none `readonly`, is built field by field** in any
     module, as a struct of another module is. `JsonOptions` is the first; the others of `std` are still
     made by their functions (`JsonError`, whose fields are `readonly`, and `Directory`, which has none).
1627. **The default `T {}` of a public function is open to the modules that call it**: one of `T`'s fields
     that is not public is refused where the default is written (`` `take` is public, and a call of another
     module cannot build a `Hidden` field by field: `b` is not public ``), before the call that would meet it.
1628. **`close` is not among the methods of 1622**: the language never calls it (`Closable` asks that the
     program does), so a `close` with a default value is a method like any other.

Tested by `ok_param_defaults`, `error_default_param_order`, `error_default_param_ref`,
`error_default_param_call`, `error_default_param_func_value`, `error_default_param_count`,
`error_default_param_null`, `error_default_param_equals`, `error_default_param_extern`,
`error_default_param_literal`, `panic_json_write_deep`, `panic_json_options`, `panic_json_options_indent`
(new), the modules `ok_param_defaults_module` and `error_default_param_hidden` (new), and by `ok_json`
(the depth of 4, 300 levels written and read back, the options kept and given again) and
`panic_json_indent`, which now write `toJson(JsonOptions { indent = n })`. The whole of the tests passes on
macOS and on Linux, with 500 of the 1914 programs compiled through C and run clean under the sanitizers,
109 of them under the thread sanitizer.

## 149. `std` measured against Rust and Zig, first: JSON

Choices made in the hundred-and-forty-ninth slice (every module of `std` measured against Rust and Zig,
first JSON). 1553 was approved during the hundred-and-thirty-sixth slice; 1629 to 1637 at the start of this
one, and 1638 to 1640, which the measurement of JSON brought, the same day (21 November 2026):

1629. **Where.** Each module is measured as the trials are, in `trials/std/<module>` with `sg/`, `rust/`
     and `zig/`, by the same `trials/measure.py` (`python3 trials/measure.py std/json`). `bench/` keeps
     its comparison with C and is not changed.
1630. **The tables are here**, in "`std`, measured against Rust and Zig" below: a short table for each
     module and what it says. The machine, the compilers and the flags are written once, in
     `trials/README.md`. (Since 21 November 2026 both are in `benchmarks/`: the tables in `library.md`, the
     rest in `method.md`.)
1631. **What is measured** is what the trials measure: SG, SG with its arithmetic checked, Rust, Zig and
     Zig with its checks (`ReleaseSafe`); the time (the best of five runs), the most memory held, the size
     of the program stripped and its lines. The command line, the environment and paths are measured for
     size and memory only (1553).
1632. **Against what**, pinned in `Cargo.lock`: `serde_json` and Zig's `std.json`; `flate2` and Zig's
     `std.compress.flate` or zlib; `zip` and Zig's `std.zip` or a library of C; `sha2` and `hmac` and
     `std.crypto`; `crc32fast` and `std.hash.Crc32`; `hex` and `base64` and `std.fmt` and `std.base64`;
     `unicode-normalization` or `icu_normalizer`; `rand`; `chrono`; `log` with `env_logger`; `std::net`
     and `std.net`. A feature that makes a crate faster and that people turn on as a rule is measured too,
     beside the default (`flate2` with `zlib-rs`), as 1554 asks.
1633. **The cases are chosen before the numbers are seen**: two to four for each module, one of a large
     input (about 100 MB), one of many small pieces of work, and one of the hard way of the module where it
     has one. The inputs are made from a seed by programs of `trials/inputs/`; nothing large is kept.
1634. **The same work, held byte for byte.** The three give the same output; where a format may lawfully

## 150. Compression and zip measured against Rust and Zig

Choices made in the hundred-and-fiftieth slice (compression and zip measured against Rust and Zig). 1642 to
1645 were approved at its start, 1646 to 1651 after the profile of the first measurement, and 1652 after a
second look at the memory of the zip reader, all on 21 November 2026:

1642. **The cases of compression**, chosen before the numbers were seen, at level 6: 100 MB of the log of
     `inputs/text.sg` compressed in gzip a piece at a time, from a file into a file; the same opened, from a
     gzip made by Python; 99,640 records of about 370 bytes (the lines of `inputs/json.sg`), each compressed
     in zlib and opened again in memory; and 100 MB that do not compress (`inputs/bytes.sg`). Each
     compressor finds its own matches, so what is compressed is not compared: `measure.py` opens what each
     wrote with Python's `gzip`, holds it to the input, and prints its size under the table. Rust is
     `flate2` twice, with its default `miniz_oxide` and with `zlib-rs`; Zig is `std.compress.flate`.
1643. **The cases of zip**: the log cut into 10,001 entries of 10,000 bytes and written deflated; the
     entries of an archive Python wrote read into memory; and an archive of 100,000 small entries listed by
     name and size. SG checks every name (1503) and Rust and Zig do not, which is said beside the table and
     not turned off. Rust is the `zip` crate with its default features, which deflate with `zlib-rs`.
1644. **Zig writes its archive by hand**: `std.zip` reads archives and does not write them, so the program
     writes the headers of `std.zip` with the DEFLATE of `std.compress.flate`, rather than a library of C
     that would compress with zlib.
1645. **Zig checks the CRC-32 itself**: `std.zip` does not check what it opens, so the program does, with
     `std.hash.Crc32`, as SG and Rust do.
1646. **A match is compared eight bytes at a time.** The search of zlib's `longest_match` took four
     fifths of the time of compressing, comparing a byte at a time; two words of eight bytes are now told
     apart by the lowest bit of their difference, with a byte at a time for the last bytes before 258. The
     length found is the one zlib finds, and so are the bytes.
1647. **A match is copied eight bytes at a time** when it lies 8 bytes back or more: each piece copies
     bytes already opened, and the last may write past the match into room the next symbols write over, so
     the output keeps 266 bytes of room, not 258. A profile had put four fifths of the time of opening in
     that loop, a byte at a time.
1648. **`List<T>.repeat(item, count)`**: `count` copies of a `Copyable` item in one block, the way to make a
     buffer whose size is known when the program runs (Rust's `vec![x; n]`, Go's `make`). An item whose
     bytes are all zero comes from `calloc`, fresh pages not written; another is written into each place;
     fewer than 0 items is a panic. `std` made its tables of 64 KB by adding 4 KB at a time, which moved
     them as they grew: setting up a compressor took two fifths of the time of the small records.
1649. **A call that compresses all at once takes the room of its input.** `Zlib.compress(read data, 6)`
     made a window of 64 KB, chains of 32,768 places and the symbols of a block for a record of 370 bytes.
     Its window, chains and symbols are now the size of its input with the 258 bytes past it that a match
     is compared over (zlib's window is zero there); the sizes zlib counts with are kept, so the bytes are
     zlib's at every level still, checked against Python's zlib at the ten levels and seventeen sizes.
     The heads of the chains are all kept, since a hash may fall anywhere.
1650. **`zip.entries()` lends the reader's list** (`read List<ZipEntry>`), as `JsonObject.values()` does
     (1640), where it copied every entry with its name. Reading an entry changes the reader, so an entry
     read while the program goes over them is copied first, with `ZipEntry.clone()`, which became public
     for it: `for i in 0..zip.entries().length() { var entry = zip.entries()[i].clone() ... }`.
1651. **What is left of the losses.** The CRC-32 of the processor's instructions, which opening gzip in
     `zlib-rs` uses, waits for the hundred-and-fifty-first slice, where CRC-32 is measured. `zlib-rs`
     compresses at level 6 by zlib-ng's own way (its "medium"), 1.7 times SG's speed for 3 per cent more
     bytes; SG keeps zlib's bytes (1485), so that is written under "Slower than C".
1652. **`List<T>.withCapacity(n)`**: an empty list with room for `n` items, as `string.withCapacity(n)`
     is for a text. The zip reader takes the room of its entries at once from the count the central
     directory gives, which the reader has checked to be at most one for 46 bytes of it, so an archive
     cannot make it take more than it holds. With it, and with three changes that change nothing a
     program sees, a reader of 100,000 entries holds about 20 MB, where it held 50: the central directory
     is read 64 KB at a time, not whole; the bytes of a name are kept apart only when the name is code page
     437; and two names of one key are found through a hash of the key (FNV-1a, in `zip.sg`), the keys of
     the two compared when their hashes agree, in place of a set of every key.

Found on the way: a slice of a list in the interpreter copied every item of the list, twice, to cut out a
few; it now copies the items it takes, and the compressor run by the interpreter, which the slices of 1646
had made five times slower, is back to its time. The search of 1646 indexes the window where it is rather
than through a slice of all of it, which the interpreter would copy at every call; compiled, the two take
the same time within 3 per cent. And `toJson` run by the interpreter took the square of the length of the
text it wrote: after each number it looked at the whole text written so far through `asBytes()`, which is a
view in compiled code and a cell for each byte in the interpreter. It looks at the number alone now, and a
megabyte of records is written back in 1.8 s, not 95 (compiled, 10 ms, where `serde_json` takes 10.5 and
Zig's `std.json` 10.3); `the_trial_of_json` no longer takes nine minutes.

Tested by `the_trial_of_compression` and `the_trial_of_zip` (new): the SG programs, interpreted and
compiled, write the same gzip and the same archive, open them back into their input and count the same
records. `ok_list_repeat`, `ok_list_with_capacity`, `panic_list_repeat_negative`,
`panic_list_capacity_negative`, `error_list_repeat_not_copyable`, `error_list_repeat_on_value`,
`error_list_function_unknown` and `error_list_reserve` are new; `ok_zip`, `panic_zip_limit` and the check of
`tests/zip-suite` copy an entry before reading it; the suite has three archives more, a name written
composed and decomposed, a name in two cases that is not ASCII, and names that only look alike, which
every version of `sg` reads alike. The whole of the tests passes on macOS and on Linux, with 504 of the 1922
programs compiled through C and run clean under the sanitizers, 109 of them under the thread sanitizer; the
tests of `tests/cli.rs` take 11 s where they took nine minutes, the most of it `the_trial_of_json`.

After the hundred-and-fiftieth slice, on 21 November 2026: `text.asBytes()` in the interpreter is a view of
the text, as it is in compiled code, where it made a cell for each byte. A byte becomes a value when it is
read; a range of the view is a view of the same text, and two views are compared where they are.
`Regex.findAll` took the bytes of its text at every match, so 20,000 matches in 200 KB took 156 s; they take
0.9 s. Compiled code is not touched, and what a program sees does not change. Tested by

## 151. SHA-256, HMAC, CRC-32 and Adler-32 measured against Rust and Zig

Choices made in the hundred-and-fifty-first slice (SHA-256, HMAC, CRC-32 and Adler-32 measured against Rust
and Zig). 1653 to 1655 were approved at its start and 1656 to 1658 after the profile of the first
measurement, all on 21 November 2026:

1653. **Two trials, as there are two modules**: `trials/std/hash` for `std/hash.sg` (SHA-256 and HMAC) and
     `trials/std/checksum` for `std/checksum.sg` (CRC-32 and Adler-32). Rust is `sha2` and `hmac`,
     `crc32fast`, and `adler2`, the Adler-32 of `miniz_oxide`, each with its default features; Zig is
     `std.crypto.hash.sha2`, `std.crypto.auth.hmac`, `std.hash.Crc32` and `std.hash.Adler32`.
1654. **The cases of hash**, chosen before the numbers were seen: the SHA-256 of 100 MB of the log, read 64
     KB at a time; 32 zero bytes hashed ten million times, each the hash of the one before, the cost of a
     call with nothing read; and each of the 921,952 lines of the log signed with HMAC-SHA-256 under one
     key, the tags put together with XOR.
1655. **The cases of checksum**: the CRC-32 and the Adler-32 of the 100 MB, read 64 KB at a time, and the
     CRC-32 of each line, put together with XOR. `Bytes.equalInConstantTime` is not measured alone.
1656. **CRC-32 by the processor's instructions.** Where it has them, a compiled program folds 64 bytes or
     more with the carry-less product (Intel's method of 2009, the keys of zlib's polynomial that
     `crc32fast` and zlib-ng use): eight streams of 16 bytes over each 128, four over each 64 when there
     are fewer, then one 16 bytes at a time; ARMv8's PMULL, with `crc32x` for what is left and what is
     shorter, and x86-64's PCLMULQDQ, with a Barrett reduction. The processor is asked once what it has
     (`sysctlbyname` on macOS, `getauxval` on Linux, `IsProcessorFeaturePresent` on Windows, `cpuid` on
     x86-64), through `System.crc32Hardware`, a call only `std` makes; where it has none, on another
     machine, without an operating system, and always in the interpreter, `std` takes sixteen bytes a step
     through its tables as before. The bytes are the same either way. Before: 0.035 s on 100 MB and 0.060
     s on the lines, against 0.010 s and 0.035 s for `crc32fast`; after, 0.013 s and 0.044 s.
1657. **SHA-256 by the processor's instructions**, the same way: ARMv8's SHA2 and x86-64's SHA-NI, four
     rounds or two at an instruction, written out so that the words of the message stay in registers,
     through `System.sha256Hardware`; the 64 rounds in SG where there are none. It takes back the word of
     1409 that `std` has no instructions made for SHA. `Sha256.of` and `Hmac.sha256` hand their blocks
     straight to the rounds and make the last block once, where they went through a `Sha256` and its
     buffer. Before: 0.303 s, 2.018 s and 1.001 s, six to nine times Rust and Zig; after, 0.049 s, 0.331 s
     and 0.138 s, and `sha256` of `bench` takes 1.17 times OpenSSL, where it took 8.93.
1658. **Adler-32 in eight lanes**: a run of up to 5,552 bytes, a whole number of words of 8, is summed with
     each byte in the lane of its place modulo 8, so that no byte waits for the sum before it, and the
     lanes give the two sums back at the end of the run, as `adler2` does with four. Plain SG, the same
     sums: 0.038 s before, 0.024 s after, against `adler2`'s 0.022 s.

What is left of the losses is written under "Slower than C": a call of SHA-256 costs 33 ns, where the
instructions take 25 and Zig's whole call 28, and the lines are read as text, checked to be UTF-8. Opening
gzip, which takes a CRC-32 of what it opens, now takes the time of `zlib-rs` (0.084 s, where it took
0.108), and reading the entries of a zip archive 0.139 s, where it took 0.164.

Tested by `the_trials_of_hash_and_checksum` (new): the SG programs, interpreted and compiled, give the
values of Python's `hashlib`, `hmac` and `zlib`. `ok_checksum_hardware` (new) takes every length from 0 to
399 from each of 16 places, lengths around the runs of Adler-32 and the folds of CRC-32 given in pieces,
and SHA-256 and HMAC around their blocks: the compiled program, which uses the instructions, prints what
the interpreter, which never does, wrote into its output, and the values were checked against Python. It
gave the same on macOS and on Linux on 64-bit Arm, built by Clang and by GCC, and on x86-64 run under
emulation, whose processor said it had PCLMULQDQ and SHA-NI; on Windows 11 on 64-bit Arm, which has the
instructions of all three, and its x64 emulation, which has PCLMULQDQ and not SHA-NI, so that the rounds
in SG ran there. The whole of the tests passes on macOS and on Linux, with 508 of the 1926 programs
compiled through C and run clean under the sanitizers, 109 of them under the thread sanitizer.

## 152. Hex, Base64 and Unicode measured against Rust and Zig

Choices made in the hundred-and-fifty-second slice (hex, Base64 and Unicode measured against Rust and Zig).
1659 to 1663 were approved at its start, 1664 to 1666 after the profile of the first measurement, all on 21
November 2026:

1659. **Two trials, as there are two modules**: `trials/std/encoding` for `std/encoding.sg` (hex and Base64)
     and `trials/std/unicode` for `std/unicode.sg` (categories and normal forms). Rust is `base64` and `hex`
     with their default features; for Unicode, ICU4X (`icu_properties`, `icu_normalizer`), the fastest of
     Rust's, built once more with the feature `unicode-normalization` for the crate of that name, the one
     most used. Zig is `std.base64` and `std.fmt`; its library has no categories and no normal forms, so its
     program calls ICU4C, as the trial of compression calls zlib, and the table says so.
1660. **The cases of encoding**, chosen before the numbers were seen: 100 MB of bytes that do not repeat
     written in Base64, read 48 KB at a time, into a file; that Base64 read back 64 KB at a time; ten
     million values of 32 bytes written as hex and read back; ten million tokens of 16 to 48 bytes in the
     Base64 of URLs and back, both made by SplitMix64. Hex of 100 MB is not measured: hex is for small
     values.
1661. **The text of Unicode**: a new maker, `trials/inputs/unicode.sg`, writes 100 MB of lines of about a
     hundred bytes of words of many writings from a seed, in NFC; Python's `unicodedata`, whose tables are
     of Unicode 17.0 as SG's are, makes its NFD, a program apart from SG.
1662. **The cases of Unicode**, each on every line: the general category of each character; the text in
     NFD into NFC and the text into NFD, written into files that must be what Python makes; and whether
     each line is in NFC. NFKC and NFKD go the same ways and are not measured.
1663. **The ways a text fails to be read** are not measured; the tests hold them.
1664. **Hex and Base64 made faster, the same bytes and errors.** A compiled `text.write(c)` of a character
     writes an ASCII one into room the text has with one store, without a call (`sg_put_char`), which
     every program has. In `std/encoding.sg` a digit is read through a table of 256 values in place of a
     row of comparisons, a list is made at the size it will have, and what is written goes through a block
     of 64 bytes on the stack, handed to the text through `string.fromUtf8` or to the list through
     `addAll` when it is full: a byte written there is one store, where a byte written into the text is a
     store and a test of its room, and the text's place in memory read again after each.
1665. **The forms that write into what the program keeps**: `Base64.encodeInto(ref text, read data)` and
     `encodeUrlInto`, beside `Hex.encodeInto`; `Hex.decodeInto(ref list, text)`, `Base64.decodeInto` and
     `decodeUrlInto`, giving `Result<void, DecodeError>`, which leave the list as it was when the text
     cannot be read. The SG trial uses them with a text and a list kept from one value to the next, as
     the programs in Rust and Zig keep their buffers.
1666. **Base64 six bytes or eight digits at a step**, each step looking at its piece of the data and of the
     block once; a group of eight digits with a wrong one in it is read again four at a time, to give the
     place the error has always had.

Before: 0.437 s, 0.635 s, 3.931 s and 4.531 s, nine to twelve times the faster of Rust and Zig; after,

tests, to a copy in Python of the code before it, on 19,000 texts that are Base64 or hex or nearly so, the
